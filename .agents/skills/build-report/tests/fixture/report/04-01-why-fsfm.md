## 4.1. Why FSFM

Face security today relies on separate specialists. Most studies treat deepfake detection (DfD) and face anti-spoofing (FAS) as separate problems, each with its own task-specific model that is hard to transfer to the other [102]. FAS is the face-specific form of PAD described in Parts 1 and 2, while DfD targets digitally manipulated faces of the kind that injection attacks deliver.

Three habits of the usual training approach limit both families. First, models are trained with full supervision, starting either from scratch or from weights learned on everyday photographs in ImageNet [102]. Second, full supervision needs large labelled collections of attacks or synthetic augmentations, which the authors describe as expensive and hard to scale [102]. Third, starting weights that carry no knowledge of faces may limit how well a network handles face-related tasks [102]. Earlier methods that learned from unlabelled faces aimed at face analysis, such as recognising expressions, and were usually tested within a single dataset, whereas security needs models that hold up across datasets [102].

FSFM makes a different bet: learn what real faces look like first, from unlabelled real faces only, and build every detector on that shared knowledge [102]. A model that understands real faces well should notice what does not fit, whether the face is a print held up to a camera, a deepfake or an AI-generated image.

Several facts made FSFM the flagship method of this report. It comes from the State Key Laboratory of Blockchain and Data Security of Zhejiang University, and it was published at CVPR 2025, pages 24364–24376 [102]; its senior author, Kui Ren, is an IEEE Fellow, an ACM Fellow and a Highly Cited Researcher [103]. Its early influence is measurable: 11 citing works in OpenAlex as of 3 October 2026, including 2026 FAS studies [104], and an independent benchmark that already uses it as a baseline [105]. Above all, it is open: the authors released the code, pretrained models, fine-tuned checkpoints and online detectors in December 2024 [106]; the pretrained model is a public file of about 1.6 GB [107]; and a public demonstration that sorts a face into real, deepfake, diffusion-generated or presentation attack loads these released weights [108]. The same group has since extended the approach to larger models in FS-VFM, posted in October 2025 [109].

Openness matters for a security method in particular, because it lets others test the claims. Section 4.4 shows that this has already happened.
