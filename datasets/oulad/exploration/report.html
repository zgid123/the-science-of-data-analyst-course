OULAD is structurally sound and ready for a deep dive, with nine data issues to handle explicitly

  • Dataset: OULAD (Open University Learning Analytics Dataset), all 7 tables, full data (10.9 million rows, no sampling).
  • Snapshot: 1 November 2026.
  • Unit of analysis: one student enrolment in one module presentation.

Status: complete descriptive exploration (lifecycle stage 5, Data Understanding). This report establishes structure, keys, relationships, quality, candidate measures and the descriptive associations of the outcome with student characteristics, early engagement, assessment behaviour and time (Section 5). Every method applied is listed with its output file in Section 8. It tests no hypothesis and estimates no causal effect; that is the deep-dive work it prepares (Section 7).

All 11 referential-integrity checks pass: every enrolment, assessment, VLE site and click record links to a valid parent, and the enrolment key is unique in both student tables. The data can therefore be joined with confidence. Nine issues need an explicit decision before analysis, because each changes a count, a denominator or a comparison (Section 3).

1. Dataset map

The seven tables describe 22 presentations (7 modules × 2013B–2014J) and 32,593 enrolments. Dates are day offsets from the start of each presentation (day 0), not calendar dates; negative values fall before the start.

Outcomes across all enrolments: Pass 12,361 (37.9%), Withdrawn 10,156 (31.2%), Fail 7,052 (21.6%) and Distinction 3,024 (9.3%). 3,538 students hold more than one enrolment, so student-level and enrolment-level counts differ.

2. Relationships

Every child table links to its parent without orphans, and every enrolment appearing in click or assessment data exists in studentInfo (results/checks.json). Two coverage facts set denominators for later analysis:

  • 3,365 enrolments (10.3%) have no VLE activity, and 6,750 (20.7%) have no assessment submission. An analysis restricted to active students therefore describes a selected population.
  • Withdrawal timing is recorded for nearly all withdrawals: 10,063 of 10,156 Withdrawn enrolments carry a date_unregistration, and 3,097 enrolments unregistered on or before day 0, before teaching began.

Natural hierarchy for slicing: module → presentation → enrolment → assessment or VLE site → day.

Scores show round-number heaping, which reflects how they are marked rather than an error. Total clicks per enrolment separate the outcome groups strongly: median 89 for Withdrawn (29.4% with no clicks), 317 for Fail, 1,343 for Pass and 1,896 for Distinction (results/segments.json). The gap is visible from the first week of teaching. In week 0 (days 0–6), enrolments that later withdrew averaged 34.7 clicks against 44.7 for Fail, 81.1 for Pass and 113.6 for Distinction, so early activity is a candidate early signal worth testing within modules (deep-dive analysis 1).

Withdrawal rates differ widely by module, from 11.5% (GGG) and 16.8% (AAA) to 44.5% (CCC), so module is a required control in any outcome comparison.

5. Outcome relationships, time and withdrawal

This section describes how the outcome (final_result) varies with student characteristics, early engagement, assessment behaviour and time. It uses one row per enrolment (results/enrolment_features.csv, 38 columns) and the summaries in results/descriptive.json, written by code/02_features.py and code/03_describe.py. Because the data hold 32,593 enrolments, almost any difference passes a significance test; the section therefore reports effect sizes and 95% intervals instead of p-values. Success means Pass or Distinction. Every statement is a descriptive association, not a cause.

5.1 Student characteristics

Success rate rises steadily with socio-economic advantage, prior education and age, and falls with repeat attempts and disability. The table below gives the lowest and highest group for each characteristic with Wilson 95% intervals, plus two association sizes: bias-corrected Cramér's V against the four-class outcome overall, and its median within the seven modules. Each association is weak on its own (V 0.06–0.12), but it keeps its size within modules, so module mix does not explain it.

The deprivation gradient is monotone apart from two small steps whose intervals overlap (30–40% to 40–50% and 60–70% to 70–80%), which makes imd_band usable as an ordinal measure (Spearman ρ with the ordered outcome 0.13).

The gender comparison reverses once module is taken into account (Simpson's paradox). Overall, women withdraw slightly less often than men (30.5% against 31.7%, −1.2 percentage points). Within modules, women withdraw more often in 6 of 7 modules (+1.4 to +7.7 points; GGG −2.4), and the module-standardised gap is +3.0 points. The reversal follows from module mix: women make up 88% of BBB and 81% of GGG, and GGG has the lowest withdrawal rate (11.5%). As a result, any gender comparison needs module as a control. An independent recount from the raw studentInfo.csv gives the same figures (code/05_crosscheck.py, logs/05_crosscheck.log). The other contrasts keep their direction and size after standardising by module: disability −10.0 points crude against −9.9 standardised, most against least deprived −18.8 against −19.1, repeat against first attempt −16.2 against −14.1 (simpson in results/descriptive.json).

Missing imd_band is not random. It is concentrated in North Region (40.1% of its enrolments) and Ireland (22.5%), giving a Cramér's V of 0.54 between missingness and region, and enrolments with a missing band succeed more often (65.7% against 46.6%). Keeping missing as its own category (issue 6) is therefore necessary, and that category partly stands for region.

Registration timing relates to withdrawal in an unexpected direction. Enrolments registered more than 100 days before the start withdraw in 38.9% of cases, against 30.3% for 31–100 days early, 25.6% for the last 30 days and 20.9% for the 234 registered after day 0, while success changes less (43.2% to 50.4%). The 45 enrolments without a registration day withdraw in 86.7% of cases. One hypothesis for the deep dive: students who enrol far ahead lose momentum before teaching starts (registration_timing).

5.2 Early engagement and assessment behaviour

To compare engagement on equal exposure, clicks are counted in the same early window, days 0–27, for every enrolment still registered on day 28 (27,553 of 32,593). The table below gives the median and middle 50% of early clicks, the median number of active days and the share with no early click, by outcome.

Final result Enrolments Median clicks, days 0–27 (middle 50%) Median active days No early click
Withdrawn (still registered on day 28) 5,124 141 (52–298) 8 5.9%
Fail 7,044 103 (27–235) 6 10.1%
Pass 12,361 217 (96–421) 11 1.6%
Distinction 3,024 299 (130–575) 14 1.0%

Successful enrolments click more in the first four weeks than unsuccessful ones, with a Cliff's delta of 0.31 for clicks and 0.34 for active days (delta is the probability that a successful enrolment ranks higher minus the probability that it ranks lower; about 0.33 is conventionally a medium effect). The effect holds in every module, from 0.26 (AAA) to 0.48 (EEE), and collapsing exact duplicate rows (issue 1) changes it by at most 0.004, so the duplicates do not affect this measure. In addition, module alone explains 21% of the variance of log early clicks (η²), which confirms module as a required control. One pattern is worth a hypothesis: students who later withdraw are more active early than students who later fail (median 141 against 103 clicks), so low early activity marks future Fail more than future withdrawal among students still enrolled at day 28.

The chart below ranks the measures by how strongly they separate successful from unsuccessful enrolments across all 32,593 enrolments (Cliff's delta; signal_ranking). Measures counted over the whole presentation lead (assessments submitted 0.83, active days 0.80, total clicks 0.75), but they partly record the outcome, because students who withdraw stop clicking and submitting. Among measures known by day 27, active days (0.50) and clicks (0.47) in days 0–27 lead, followed by clicks before day 0 (0.36), which are available before teaching starts, and the first assessment score (0.34). Background measures separate weakly: prior education 0.16, IMD band 0.15 and studied credits −0.14 (a heavier workload goes with lower success). The early-window deltas here are larger than the 0.31 of the equal-exposure comparison above, because they include students who left before day 28.

The first assessment is the clearest early signal. Of Withdrawn enrolments, 55.4% never submitted the first TMA or CMA of their presentation, against 20.9% of Fail, 1.0% of Pass and 0.2% of Distinction; among those who submitted, the first score separates success with a Cliff's delta of 0.34. This comparison mixes warning and consequence: 3,089 Withdrawn enrolments left on or before day 0, and 22.8% of the withdrawals during the presentation happen before the first due day, so their non-submission follows from leaving. A clean early-warning measure counts non-submission only among students still registered on the due day (deep-dive analysis 3).

Assessment data have three further properties to plan around. First, only 6 of 24 exams have any score records (DDD all four, CCC two of four), so exam performance is observable for two modules only (issue 7). Second, the computer-marked assessments of BBB and GGG sit at a ceiling (median score 100), so their scores separate students poorly. Third, late submissions are mostly a few days late: median 3 days, 90th percentile 9, 99th percentile 29 (assessments in results/descriptive.json).

The mix of activity differs only slightly by outcome. Forum use (forumng) takes 28.8% of successful students' early clicks against 24.6% for unsuccessful ones (+4.2 points), while quizzes (−1.7) and subpages (−2.0) take a larger share for unsuccessful students (activity_mix).

5.3 Withdrawal over time

Withdrawal is spread over the whole presentation rather than concentrated at the start. Of the 29,403 enrolments still registered on day 0, the Kaplan–Meier estimate shows 6.7% withdrawn by day 28, 10.7% by day 60, 16.1% by day 120 and 23.7% by the end, with the rest censored at the presentation end; the 3,097 who left on or before day 0 and the 93 withdrawals without a date are outside this curve (survival). Half of the in-presentation withdrawals happen by day 73 (quartiles at days 26 and 142). Retention at day 60 ranges from 81.4% in CCC to 97.4% in GGG, and at the end from 62.9% to 91.0%.

Daily activity follows a weekly rhythm and peaks around assessments. Across the 22 presentations, the median autocorrelation of daily clicks at a 7-day lag is 0.53, and the three days ending on a TMA or CMA due day carry 2.2 times the clicks of other days (median; 1.7–3.2 across presentations, highest in CCC) (temporal).

Withdrawal rose from 2013 to 2014 in all 9 module-semester pairs observed in both years, by 0.7 to 9.9 points (largest in GGG J), while success changed by between −4.9 and +2.3 points (temporal.year_on_year). Year is therefore a required control or split: a model fitted on 2013 and tested on 2014 faces a higher base rate of withdrawal. B (February) presentations also succeed less than J (October) ones, 5.7 points crude and 3.9 points within modules.

5.4 Repeat enrolments and course design

Of the 3,538 students with more than one enrolment, 1,081 pairs of enrolments run in the same presentation period (concurrent modules) and 2,727 follow one another, 1,201 of them a repeat of the same module. The attempt counter num_of_prev_attempts rises at every same-module repeat (1,201 of 1,201), so it is consistent with the enrolment history. A repeat after Withdrawn or Fail ends in success 27.6% of the time (25.2–30.2%), well below the 47.2% of all enrolments, and 615 of the 1,322 enrolments that follow a withdrawal (46.5%) end in withdrawal again (repeat_transitions).

Use of the learning environment is concentrated in few sites. Of 6,364 VLE sites, 96 (1.5%) were never clicked, while the top 10% of sites draw 86.9% of all clicks (Gini 0.90). Content pages (oucontent, 28.3% of clicks), forums (20.1%), quizzes (17.6%) and the homepage (17.6%) carry most activity, whereas resource sites make up 42% of all sites (2,660) but draw 2.8% of clicks. Presentations also differ in design, for example 5 TMA and CMA in AAA against 11 in BBB 2013B, so site and assessment counts describe the module rather than the student (course_design).

5.5 Distribution shape, correlation and redundancy

Activity is concentrated in a minority of enrolments: the top 10% generate 43% of all clicks (Gini 0.63), total clicks have a skewness of 3.0, and 2,503 enrolments (7.7%) lie beyond the interquartile-range fence. These are heavy users rather than errors, so the deep dive should keep them and use medians, ranks or a log scale; the log transform over-corrects (skewness −1.2) because of the zero-click enrolments (univariate).

Three features duplicate one another or the outcome. Total clicks and active days correlate at Spearman ρ 0.95 (0.91 within the early window), so one of each pair is enough. The number of submitted assessments correlates with the ordered outcome at ρ 0.74, but it partly records the outcome itself (withdrawn students stop submitting), so it is usable as a predictor only when counted inside a fixed early window. In the source tables, no column is constant, no text label differs only by case or spacing, and vle.week_to equals week_from for 99.1% of the sites that carry a week, so one of the two is enough (structure, correlations).

5.6 Confounding, interactions and multivariate structure

This subsection uses results/multivariate.json and results/trajectory_clusters.csv, written by code/04_multivariate.py. The student and course dimensions are largely independent of one another, with two exceptions that matter for comparisons (dimension_associations). Module and gender are strongly associated (Cramér's V 0.60), which produces the gender reversal in Section 5.1. Module and year are moderately associated (0.35), because not every module ran in both years (CCC only in 2014), so year comparisons need module as a control. Of the 45 pairs, 9 reach V 0.10, and all except these two stay below 0.17.

The deprivation gradient is not explained by engagement or prior education. Among students registered on day 28, the most deprived fifth succeed 17.9 points less often than the least deprived fifth; within tertiles of early clicks the gap is 14.7 to 16.5 points (15.4 after standardising), and within education levels it is 14.2 to 24.9 points. Early engagement therefore accounts for about a seventh of the gap at most. The disability gap behaves similarly (−10.7 points crude, −9.5 within engagement tertiles), except among the least engaged, where it narrows to −5.1 (stratified_gradients).

The gradients differ in size between modules but not in direction: the deprivation gap ranges from −5.7 points (AAA, the smallest module) to −22.2 (BBB), and the disability gap from −4.8 (GGG) to −19.7 (AAA). Year and semester change them little (deprivation −20.5 in 2013 against −17.6 in 2014), and the early-engagement effect is the same in both years (Cliff's delta 0.31 and 0.32) (interactions).

The measures known by day 27 carry several separate dimensions rather than one. A principal component analysis on the ranks of 10 such measures needs four components to explain 64% of the variance: early engagement with first-assessment submission (30%; it separates success with a Cliff's delta of 0.55), workload and history (13%: studied credits, previous attempts, earlier registration; −0.14), background (11%: education, IMD band, age; 0.02) and registration timing with previous attempts (10%). As a result, background and engagement measures add information to each other in a model rather than repeating it (pca).

Clustering the weekly click trajectories gives three groups, shown in the chart below (trajectories; k-means on log clicks in weeks 0–37, with k chosen as the highest silhouette among 3–6 before the clusters were inspected). A low-activity group (42% of enrolments) fades out within the first weeks and succeeds 8.6% of the time, a middle group (33%) succeeds 63.2% and a sustained high-activity group (24%) succeeds 92.7%. The clusters describe a continuum rather than distinct types (silhouette 0.26; k = 2 scored 0.36 but only separates active from inactive students), and because the trajectories run to the end of the presentation they partly record withdrawal itself (Cramér's V with the outcome 0.52). They therefore summarise engagement descriptively; an early-warning version would cluster weeks 0–3 only.

6. Recommended dimensions and measures

  • Dimensions (3–50 values): code_module (7), code_presentation (4), assessment_type (3), activity_type (20), region (13), highest_education (5), imd_band (10 + missing), age_band (3), gender (2), disability (2).
  • Outcome: final_result (4 classes), often grouped as Pass/Distinction vs Fail/Withdrawn.
  • Measures: assessment score, submission lateness (days after due), clicks per student × day aggregated to a window, days of activity, num_of_prev_attempts, studied_credits.
  • Time: day offsets in date, date_submitted, date_registration, date_unregistration.
  • Join keys: enrolment (code_module, code_presentation, id_student); id_assessment; id_site.

7. Recommended deep-dive analyses

  1. Early engagement and outcome, within module: model success on days 0–27 active days and clicks with module and year as controls; the descriptive effect (Cliff's delta 0.26–0.48 by module, Section 5.2) sets the size to expect. Test the hypothesis that low early activity marks Fail more than withdrawal.
  2. Time to withdrawal: extend the Kaplan–Meier description (Section 5.3) to a Cox or discrete-time model on date_unregistration, with Pass/Fail censored at the presentation end, module and year as strata, and first-assessment submission as a time-varying covariate (skills: scikit-survival, statsmodels).
  3. Assessment behaviour as an early signal: missing or late first TMA/CMA against later Fail/Withdrawn, respecting module weighting rules (issue 4) and the due-day definition (issue 5).
  4. Socio-economic gradient: outcome by imd_band and highest_education, controlling for module and region, with the missing band kept as a category; report the gender gap only within module, given the Simpson reversal (Section 5.1; statistical-analysis).
  5. Repeat attempts: outcomes of the 3,538 students with several enrolments, separating prior attempts (num_of_prev_attempts) from banked results.

Reproduce

From the repository root:

venv/bin/python scripts/fetch-datasets/fetch_datasets.py                       # OULAD, hash-verified
venv/bin/pip install -r datasets/oulad/exploration/requirements.txt
bash datasets/oulad/exploration/code/run_all.sh                   # every step, output in logs/

run_all.sh runs the pipeline stage by stage: dataset check, code/01_profile.py (structure, columns, checks), code/02_features.py (one row per enrolment), code/03_describe.py (segments and Sections 5.1–5.5), code/04_multivariate.py (Section 5.6), code/05_crosscheck.py, code/06_kdense_audits.sh (writes evidence/) and code/07_build_report.py (writes report.html). It saves each stage's command, timestamp and console output in logs/<stage>.log; the stage table is in README.md.

Skills applied: data-analyst (lifecycle stage and verification ledger), explore-data (structure, profile, quality framework, relationships, recommendations) and exploratory-data-analysis (scripted profiles, leakage and distribution-sensitivity audits in evidence/).
