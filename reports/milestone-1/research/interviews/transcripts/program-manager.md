**Interviewer (opening prompt, brief 4.2):** see scenario.md, role = PM – Academic Program Manager.

**Interviewer (Q1):** Looking at the online and blended courses in your program, what would you most like to improve over the next year, and why does it matter to the program?

**PM:** Honestly, what I most want to fix is that we find out too late. Today I get pass and withdrawal counts per course from the academic management system, but only after the semester closes. By then the student has already failed or quietly disappeared, and all I can do is put it in the report. In the blended courses especially, my impression (and it is only an impression, I have no figure for it) is that some students stop following the online part around mid-semester, and nobody notices until the exam. I would like the program to know earlier and act earlier, through advisors, homeroom teachers or the lecturer, whoever is right for the case. Why it matters: dropout and on-time graduation are indicators the Ministry watches, and the first-year dropout threshold affects our enrolment quota. The new circular takes effect later this month and changes who counts as a dropout. But let me be clear: I don't want a lower rate on paper. I want students supported to meet the standard, not the standard lowered.

**Interviewer (Q1 follow-up):** You said the program finds out too late. In practice, how early would the program need to know, and who would need to know, for it to still make a difference within the semester?

**PM:** In my experience, if we hear about a student only after the mid-term exam, it is already very late. My own view, and this is an opinion rather than something we have measured, is that the first four or five weeks of a semester are when it still makes a difference: the student can catch up, change study habits, or we can talk about approved leave before it turns into an unexplained absence. For first-year students I would want it even earlier, because adapting to university is where many of them struggle. As for who needs to know: first the advisor or homeroom teacher, because they can actually call the student. The lecturer, of course, but lecturers carry heavy loads and not all of them will act, so I can't rely on them alone. Student affairs when it is a financial or personal problem. And me, but at program level, not student by student, so I can see which courses keep producing these cases and decide where to put support.

**Interviewer (Q3):** How do you currently judge whether a course or a cohort is doing well or badly? What figures or reports do you look at, and how often?

**PM:** Mostly from what the academic management system gives me after each semester: pass and withdrawal counts per course, and grade results. Once a year I look at the cohort figures we report upward, meaning dropout, retention and on-time graduation by cohort, because those go into the university's annual report and the Ministry's standard has floors and ceilings on them. I also read them for accreditation self-studies and program reviews. So in practice my rhythm is once per semester for courses and once per year for cohorts, and both are looking backwards. What I don't have is anything during the semester; I hear about a bad course from a lecturer complaining or from students coming to the office. I'll be honest that I'm not fully comfortable judging a course on dropout alone. A course can have low withdrawal and still teach badly, and I also doubt that time spent online on the LMS tells me whether anyone learned anything. That's my own view, not something we have tested.

**Interviewer (Q5):** Which decisions about courses, staff or student support are yours to make, and which would you make differently if you had better information?

**PM:** Within the program, a few things are mine. I decide, with the dean's approval, how advisors and homeroom teachers are assigned and how many students each one carries. I lead course and curriculum redesign when something keeps going wrong, and I decide where extra support goes, for example more English modules or more advising for first-year students. I also have to coordinate any early-warning effort with other faculties, and get lecturers to actually take part, which is not easy. Admissions quotas and the student-status rules are not mine; those sit with the academic affairs office and the university. What would I do differently? Today I spread support more or less evenly, because I can't see where it is needed. With better information I would put advisors' time on the courses and groups that need it, and act within the semester instead of after it. But I want to be careful: I have read that smaller caseloads go with somewhat better retention, and that is an association, not proof. I wouldn't move staff on a single number.

**Interviewer (Q5 follow-up):** You said you would not move staff on a single number. What would you need to see before moving advisors' time to a particular course or group?

**PM:** First, I'd want to see that the problem repeats, not one bad semester or one difficult lecturer; if the same course or the same group of students struggles term after term, I take it seriously. Second, the records must be right. If a student on approved leave or who transferred programs is counted as a dropout, the picture is wrong, and with the new circular that distinction matters even more. Third, it has to agree with what people on the ground tell me: the lecturer, the advisors, the students themselves. If the figures say one thing and everyone in the room says another, I want to understand why before I move anyone. And I'm wary of easy explanations. I remember the debate about a well-known early-warning system whose retention gain turned out to reflect mostly how many courses students took. So I'd want to know the difference isn't simply because one group is different to begin with. Finally, if we do move advisors' time, I'd want to check afterwards whether it helped, rather than assume it did.

**Interviewer (Q6):** What resources could you realistically move or add to support students during a semester, and what limits them?

**PM:** Realistically, not much new money appears mid-semester, so it is mostly about moving what I have. I can ask advisors and homeroom teachers to spend more of their time on certain students or courses, I can arrange extra tutoring or consultation hours, often with teaching assistants or senior students, and I can refer students to student affairs for financial or personal problems. Extra English support or a new module is something I plan for the next semester, not the current one. The limits are mainly people and time. Advisors and homeroom teachers do this on top of teaching, and my impression, which I haven't counted, is that each already carries more students than they can follow closely. Lecturers can't be told to do extra work; I have to persuade them, and some see this as administration rather than teaching. Budget changes go through the dean and the university, so they move on a yearly cycle. And some things I simply can't fix, like students who work long hours or chose the wrong major.

**Interviewer (Q7):** What would make you distrust or ignore the results of an analysis like this?

**PM:** Several things. If the student-status records behind it are wrong, for example students on approved leave or who transferred counted as dropouts, I would stop reading right there. If a result makes our rate look better only because the counting changed, I'd distrust it immediately; a lower rate on paper is not better quality. I'm also suspicious of anything that treats time on the LMS as if it were learning, because in my experience students can log in and leave a page open. If the results come only as one score or a list of names without saying why, I can't defend them to the dean or to a lecturer, so I'd probably ignore them. Big promises make me cautious too; I've seen vendor claims of retention gains that turned out to be about something else. And if it flags many students but nobody has time to contact them, it will be ignored in practice, however good it is. Finally, if it contradicts what advisors see and nobody can explain why, I'd trust the advisors first.

**Interviewer (Q8):** What could go wrong for students or staff if the university acts on such results?

**PM:** For students, the first worry is labelling. If a student is called "at risk" and a lecturer starts treating them that way, it can become a self-fulfilling thing, and a first-year student who is just slow to adapt may feel pushed out rather than helped. Some students will be flagged wrongly, and a call from an advisor can feel like surveillance if it isn't handled kindly. Others who really struggle but don't show it online will be missed, and we might feel falsely reassured. There is also privacy: students and parents may not expect their LMS activity to be watched, and I assume we'd need clear rules on who sees what. For staff, I worry the results get used to judge lecturers whose courses are simply harder, which would make them resist the whole effort. Advisors could be handed long lists on top of their teaching with no extra time. And at university level, there's the temptation to manage the number instead of the students, for example by reclassifying cases, which I've been warned against and which I don't want.
