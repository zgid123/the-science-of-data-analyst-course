# Interview transcript: CL – Course Lecturer

Separate conversation with an isolated AI subagent playing the persona in personas/lecturer.md (T09, 2026-11-05). Interviewer: the team (Data Analytics Team). Questions from questions/lecturer.md; follow-ups marked "follow-up". Answers are written to this file by the persona agent, verbatim.

**Interviewer (opening prompt, brief 4.2):** see scenario.md, role = CL – Course Lecturer.

**Interviewer (Q1):** In your online or blended courses, what outcome for your students matters most to you over a semester?

**CL:** Honestly? That they're still with me at the end. I coordinate a big first-year blended course, around 250 students across four sections, and the thing that keeps me up is not the final grade distribution, it's the students who quietly stop showing up. In my experience we lose them somewhere between the second and third assignment, or the third and fourth. They don't announce it. They just go silent: no forum post, a missed small quiz, then a late submission, then nothing. So for me the outcome that matters most is that a student who starts the course actually finishes it, and finishes having done the work, not just logged in. Online that's harder, because, you know, cameras off, mics off, someone can be "present" and be doing something else entirely. I'd add, and this is my own view, not something I've measured: passing matters, but staying engaged long enough to get help is what makes passing possible. If I can reach them early, most of the rest follows.

**Interviewer (Q1 follow-up):** You said you want students to finish having done the work, not just logged in. At the end of a semester, what would show you that this happened?

**CL:** Good question, and I'll be honest, I don't have one clean signal for it. What I'd look at, in my own view, is whether the trail of work is complete. Did they submit the assignments, all of them, not just the big final one? Did they do the low-stakes quizzes along the way, the weekly ones that barely count? A student who skips those and then turns up for the exam, that's someone who was logged in but not really doing the work. I'd also look at whether the work came in roughly on time across the semester, rather than a pile of late submissions at the end. Right now I track a lot of that in my own spreadsheet, who submitted what, and it's patchy with 250 students. Grades matter too, of course, but a pass on its own doesn't tell me they engaged. And I'd be careful with login time; I assume plenty of students leave the LMS open and walk away, and some work offline. So clicks alone wouldn't convince me.

**Interviewer (Q3):** When you notice a student going silent, what happens next? What do you do, and what gets in the way?

**CL:** Mostly it's me, by hand. I email first. If nothing comes back, I'll try a phone call, because honestly you can read a lot more from someone's voice than from an email. And very often there's just no answer. I contacted them, no reply, left a voice message, and that's where it stops. Sometimes I refer them to student support, but every referral is more paperwork for me, and I don't always hear what happened afterwards. My two TAs help, but with 250 students the follow-up is the part that eats the time. What gets in the way? Time, first. Then timing: by the time I'm sure someone's gone quiet, it's often already week five or six, and in my experience that can be too late. And I try not to assume they're lazy. A lot of our students don't have a quiet room or decent internet, and some would rather disappear than lose face by admitting they're struggling. That's my impression, not something I've checked. So a silent student isn't always a disengaged one.

**Interviewer (Q5):** If you had better information about how your students are doing during the semester, what would you do differently, and when?

**CL:** The big change would be timing. I'd want to reach out in the first two or three weeks, not week six. In practice I'd probably look right before each assignment deadline, especially the second and third ones, because in my experience that's where we lose them. If I could see who has gone quiet a few days before a deadline, I'd send a short personal note, or ask a TA to call, while there's still something to rescue. Second, I'd stop keeping my own spreadsheet if something else tracked who submitted what and what I'd already done about it: who I emailed, who answered, who I referred. That follow-up record is the part I lose track of. Third, in class, I might change how I run the first weeks, for example asking for an introduction post early so silence shows up sooner. That's my own idea, I haven't tried it at this scale. But I'll push back a little: better information only helps if I have the time to act on it. Another report I don't have hours to read won't change anything.

**Interviewer (Q6):** Which parts of the course are you free to change during a semester, and which are fixed?

**CL:** I should say up front that this is how it works for me in practice; I'm describing my own understanding, not quoting the regulations. What's fixed, as I understand it, is the skeleton: the learning outcomes, the assessment weights, the number of credits, the 15 weeks, and the exam period. Once the syllabus is approved and published to students, I can't move a major assignment's weight or drop the final exam halfway through. That would need approval, and students would rightly complain. Since it's a coordinated course with four sections, the common assessments also have to stay the same across sections, so I can't change them on my own. What I'm free to change is quite a lot of the day-to-day: how I use the in-class sessions, extra practice quizzes that don't count much, the forum activities, reminders, who my TAs contact and when, extra consultation hours. I can sometimes give an individual extension too. So I can change how I support students fairly freely, but not what they're assessed on.

**Interviewer (Q7):** What would make you stop trusting or using information about your students that came from an analysis?

**CL:** The quickest way to lose me is to flag students I know are fine. If it tells me a student is at risk and I know she's doing the readings offline and handing in solid work, I'll stop reading it after the second or third time. It's not always going to be right, I accept that, people work offline, but if it's wrong about the students I know, why would I believe it about the ones I don't? Second, if I can't see why someone was flagged. A red dot with no reason is useless to me; I need to know it's, say, two missed quizzes, so I know what to say when I call. Third, if it arrives too late, after I've already noticed myself, then it adds nothing. And fourth, and this matters to me personally, if I suspected it would be used to judge my teaching rather than to help students. In my view, the moment lecturers think the numbers feed into their evaluation, they'll stop engaging with it honestly.

**Interviewer (Q8):** How much extra time could you give to follow-up work in a typical week, and what would have to give way?

**CL:** Realistically? Maybe an hour, an hour and a half a week on top of what I already do, and that's a rough guess on my part, not something I've timed. In the busy weeks around deadlines and marking, close to nothing. My TAs could take some of it, perhaps a couple of hours each, but they're also marking for 250 students, so it's not free time either. What would give way? Honestly, it would come out of my research time first, and that's already the part that gets squeezed every semester. I'd rather not cut consultation hours, because that's where struggling students do come to me. So for me the question isn't only how much time, it's how short each follow-up is. If I can open a list, see five names and the reason for each, and send a message in ten minutes, I'll do that every week. If it means logging into three systems and cross-checking my spreadsheet, it won't happen, however good the intention.
