# Interview transcript: AD – Academic Advisor / Student Support Officer

Separate conversation with an isolated AI subagent playing the persona in personas/advisor.md (T09, 2026-11-05). Interviewer: the team (Data Analytics Team). Questions from questions/advisor.md; follow-ups marked "follow-up". Answers are written to this file by the persona agent, verbatim.

**Interviewer (opening prompt, brief 4.2):** see scenario.md, role = AD – Academic Advisor / Student Support Officer.

**Interviewer (Q1):** What do you most want to achieve for the students you support during a semester?

**AD:** Honestly, what I want most is to reach the students who are slipping before they land on the academic warning list. Right now I mostly meet them after the semester, when the list comes out, and by then the failed modules are already on their record. In my experience, the students who struggle rarely come to us on their own; they just go quiet. So my aim is simple: every student who starts drifting should get a real conversation with someone who knows them, ideally their own cố vấn học tập, early enough that it can still change the semester. A flag on its own doesn't keep anyone in a course; the follow-up does. The second thing I want is for our advisors not to burn out doing it. Most of them are lecturers advising one or two classes of 60 to 80 students on top of teaching, and our office is three people for about four thousand students. So whatever we do has to fit that capacity, not add another pile of emails nobody has time to answer.

**Interviewer (Q1 follow-up):** You mentioned three officers for about four thousand students. If students who are drifting could be identified earlier, how many could your office and the advisors realistically contact and follow up in a typical week?

**AD:** I have to be careful here, because we don't keep a shared record of contacts, so this is my own estimate, not a counted figure. A proper follow-up, meaning a call, maybe a second try, then a meeting if the student answers, takes real time. I'd guess each of us three officers could manage perhaps ten to fifteen of those in a normal week, alongside everything else the office does. The lecturer-advisors know their own classes better, but they're teaching, so I'd assume a handful each, say three to five students a week, and fewer in exam or registration weeks, which is exactly when problems pile up. Beyond that, the honest answer is email, and in my experience an email from someone the student doesn't know mostly goes unread. So if a list came to us with two hundred names in one week, we couldn't do it justice. I'd rather get a shorter list we can actually act on, with some sense of who is most urgent.

**Interviewer (Q3):** What is the hardest part of reaching and helping a student who has stopped taking part in a course?

**AD:** The hardest part isn't finding out, it's the follow-up. When a lecturer emails me that a student has disappeared, I write, I call, and very often nobody answers. Students who've stopped coming are usually avoiding the subject altogether; in my experience they rarely come forward by themselves, and a message from our office feels like a warning, not help. They reply far more often when it comes from their own cố vấn học tập, someone they already know, but those advisors are lecturers with very little spare time. The second hard part is that by the time the news reaches me it's often late. The lecturer notices weeks of absence, maybe mentions it, and the student is already behind in several modules, not just one. And then, when I do get through, the reasons are rarely academic only: money, a part-time job, family, sometimes health. I can listen and refer them on, but I can't fix those things myself. Finally, I often don't close the loop with the lecturer who told me, and I think that's why some stop telling me at all.

**Interviewer (Q4):** How would you know that your support work had made a difference by the end of a semester?

**AD:** Honestly, right now I mostly don't know, and that bothers me. We don't keep a shared record of who we contacted, so at the end of the semester I can't line up the students we reached against the warning list. What I'd like to see is that the students we talked to early didn't end up on that list, or at least finished the courses they were in danger of dropping. But I'm wary of fooling myself. The students who answer my calls and come to a meeting are often the ones who were going to pull through anyway; the ones who never reply are a different group. So if someone tells me "students who met an advisor did much better", I'd want to know whether that's our help or just who chose to show up. Smaller signs matter to me too: a student who comes back to class, a lecturer who tells me it helped and keeps sending names. That's my own view of it, though; I haven't seen it measured here.

**Interviewer (Q5):** When you have more students to contact than time allows, how do you decide whom to contact first?

**AD:** Mostly by gut feeling, if I'm honest, and by what the lecturer wrote. I can call the urgent ones; the rest get an email. Urgent, for me, is a student who's vanished from several courses at once, not just one, or someone already on the academic warning list from last semester, because a second warning gets serious. First-years come high too; they haven't learned how the system works and they drop off quietly. If a lecturer mentions something personal, family trouble, money, health, that goes near the top. After that it's partly who shouted loudest, which I know isn't fair. A short, worried email from one lecturer can get more of my attention than a quiet student in a class where nobody reports anything. And in registration week everything piles up together, so some names just wait. I don't think my way is wrong, but it isn't written down anywhere, and two officers in our office might well pick differently from the same list.

**Interviewer (Q6):** What kinds of support can you actually offer, and which work best for which students?

**AD:** Less than people imagine. Mainly it's a conversation: a call or a meeting where we work out what's going on, then a plan for the semester, which modules to catch up first, whether to talk to the lecturer about a late submission, sometimes whether to drop a course before it becomes a fail. Beyond that I refer: to the lecturer, to the faculty for registration questions, to whatever financial or counselling help the university has, though I'd say that part depends on what's available that year. In my experience, first-years respond best to their own cố vấn học tập checking in early, simply because someone noticed. Students who are behind academically but still engaged do well with a concrete catch-up plan and a word from the lecturer. The hardest group are students with money or family pressure, working long hours; there the honest help is often about reducing their load, not motivating them. What works least, I think, is a generic reminder email; students learn to ignore those quickly. That's my impression from the cases I've handled, not something we've ever measured.

**Interviewer (Q7):** What could go wrong when a student is contacted because of information about their study activity?

**AD:** Quite a lot, I think. The first worry is that the student feels watched. If I call and say "we saw you haven't logged in for two weeks", some will hear surveillance, not care, especially if nobody told them that information was being looked at. I'd want students to know the purpose up front, and a clear reason before we act on it personally. Second, the information can simply be wrong or incomplete: a student studies from downloaded files, or works in a group on a friend's account, and I end up accusing someone who's fine. That damages trust with them and with their classmates. Third, labels stick. If a name lands on a list, I worry lecturers start seeing that student differently. Fourth is contact fatigue: if every small dip triggers a message, students stop reading anything from us, including the messages that matter. And last, I don't want the system deciding for us. My colleagues and I know things a screen doesn't, and the judgement about whether and how to contact someone should stay with a person. That's my view; I haven't seen these risks studied here.

**Interviewer (Q8):** What would make you or your colleagues stop using a list of students to contact?

**AD:** A few things, and I've seen versions of them with lecturers' alerts already. The quickest way to lose us is a list that's too long. If it comes with two hundred names and three of us, we'll call the first few and quietly stop opening it. Second, if we call the first names and they're fine, the student was ill for a week, or studies offline, then after a couple of those we stop trusting it. Third, timing: a list that lands in registration or exam week, when we're already drowning, will be ignored, however good it is. Fourth, if it tells us only that someone is "at risk" but not why, I can't start a conversation from that, so it's not much use to me. And honestly, if it's one more system to log into, separate from our email and the warning lists, my colleagues who advise on top of teaching won't use it. Finally, if nobody ever asks us whether it helped, or nothing changes when we say it doesn't, we'll drift back to our own way. That's my own expectation, not something we've tested here.
