# Interview transcript: ST – Student

Separate conversation with an isolated AI subagent playing the persona in personas/student.md (T09, 2026-11-05). Interviewer: the team (Data Analytics Team). Questions from questions/student.md; follow-ups marked "follow-up". Answers are written to this file by the persona agent, verbatim.

**Interviewer (opening prompt, brief 4.2):** see scenario.md, role = ST – Student.

**Interviewer (Q1):** What matters most to you when you take an online or blended course?

**ST:** Honestly, what matters most to me is that the course fits around my life. I work at a café four or five evenings a week, so I need a predictable rhythm, like materials go up on Monday, discussion by midweek, quiz on Friday. When due dates keep shifting or the course page is confusing, I lose hours just working out what I'm supposed to do. Second is some flexibility when things go wrong. In my second year I took extra shifts and someone in my family got sick back home, I fell a few weeks behind in a blended course, and after that it felt too late to catch up, so I failed it. I think a simple extension would have changed that. And third, I want to feel like a person, not just an account on the screen, so a lecturer who actually answers emails. That's my own experience and what I hear from a few classmates, not every student.

**Interviewer (Q1 follow-up):** You said a simple extension might have changed things. At that time, did you ask anyone for one? What happened, or what stopped you?

**ST:** No, I didn't, not properly. At first I thought I'd catch up on the weekend, so it didn't feel serious enough to bother anyone. Then a couple of weeks went by and I was too embarrassed. In a big class the lecturer doesn't really know my face, so writing to say "I'm behind" felt like admitting I couldn't handle it, and I honestly wasn't sure who I was supposed to ask, the lecturer, the faculty office, someone else? I did send one email near the end, quite short, and I never got a reply. Maybe it went to the wrong person, I don't know. After that I just stopped trying and thought maybe I'm not cut out for this. Looking back, I think if the process for asking had been clear and simple, I would have asked much earlier. That's my own experience; I can't say how it works for other students.

**Interviewer (Q3):** Looking back, when did it start, and who noticed – you, a friend, a lecturer, anyone at the university?

**ST:** I think it started around week four or five, when I picked up extra shifts and then the news came that someone at home was sick. First I skipped one in-person session because I was exhausted, then I stopped doing the readings and just clicked through the online part so it looked like I'd been there. Honestly, I noticed it myself before anyone else did, I just kept telling myself I'd fix it next week. A friend in the class asked once why I hadn't come, but she was busy too, so it didn't go further. As far as I know, no lecturer or anyone from the university noticed, or if they did, nobody said anything to me. I only heard from the course when the results came out. I'm not sure what they could see on their side; that's just how it felt to me, like nobody was looking.

**Interviewer (Q4):** What would have helped you, or your classmate, finish that course?

**ST:** For me, practical things more than encouragement. A clear, simple way to get an extension, or to pause a couple of weeks and pick up again without losing everything, would have helped most. And a fixed weekly rhythm, so I could plan around my shifts. I also think one personal message would have made a difference, something like "I see you've missed a few weeks, can we talk?" from the lecturer, not a scary "you are at risk" warning. That kind of message might have made me give up faster, honestly. Even a short session at a later time, after my shift, would have been enough. For my classmate, I can only guess. She stopped attending the online course halfway and withdrew. I think she felt alone in it, just an account on a screen, but I never asked her the real reason, so that's my assumption, not something she told me.

**Interviewer (Q5):** If the university noticed you were falling behind, how would you want them to contact you, and who should it be?

**ST:** For me, the lecturer of that course, or a tutor who actually knows the course, not an office I've never heard of. A private message, short and normal, like "I noticed you've missed a few things, is everything okay? Here are your options," with a real way to reply, maybe a short meeting after my shift. Not in front of the class, I'd feel like I'm losing face. And once is enough; if it keeps coming every week, it feels like they're constantly watching me, and we're adults. I should say my classmates don't all agree. One friend would prefer a neutral email that goes to everyone listing the support options, so nobody is singled out. Also, please don't judge from the system alone. I logged in plenty that semester and still wasn't really studying, so I think they should ask before assuming. That's my own view.

**Interviewer (Q6):** What would you think if you learned the university used your study activity in the online system to decide whom to contact?

**ST:** Honestly, mixed feelings. If it's only to find people who might need help and offer it, I think I'd be okay with it, because in my case nobody noticed at all. But I'd want to be told at the start of the course that they look at it, not find out later. It would feel a bit creepy otherwise. My bigger worry is that the system doesn't show what's really happening. Some of my classmates log in just so they don't get banned from the exam for absences, then turn the camera off and do other things. And some of us study from a shared room on an old phone with weak internet, so we might look less active when we're actually trying. So I'd want them to ask, not decide. And it should never count against my grade or end up on some record. That's my personal view, not something I know about how the university works.

**Interviewer (Q7):** What would make such contact feel helpful, and what would make it feel unwelcome?

**ST:** Helpful would be if it comes from someone who knows me a little, sounds like a normal person, and offers something real, like a new deadline, a way to pause, or ten minutes after my shift. If it just says "please work harder" or sends me to a website, that's not help. Timing matters too. Week four, I could still have caught up. By week ten it already felt too late. Unwelcome is anything that sounds like a label, "you are at risk" or "you are failing". I think that would make me feel like I've already lost and just quit. Same if it's in front of other people, or if my parents or my employer get told somehow. And if messages keep coming every few days, it feels like being watched, not cared about. Some classmates might feel differently, but for me it's about being treated like an adult with a life outside class.

**Interviewer (Q7 follow-up):** You said that in week four you could still have caught up. Thinking of weeks two to five, what changed in what you did for the course, week by week?

**ST:** I'll try, but it's from memory, so the exact weeks might be a bit off. Weeks two and three were normal. I went to the in-person sessions, did most of the readings, and did the online quiz, even if sometimes late at night after work. Around week four the café gave me extra shifts, so I was tired. I skipped one in-person session, and I did the quiz quickly on my phone without really reading the material first. Then in week five the news came from home that someone in my family was sick, and I was calling home a lot and worrying. That week I stopped doing the readings, didn't post in the discussion, and just opened the online pages and clicked through so it looked like I'd been there. I think the quiz that week was the first one I missed completely. After that it just kept sliding.
