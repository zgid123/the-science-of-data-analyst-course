# Persona notes: Student

Grounding for the AI-simulated Student persona (task T05). Each line is a trait, experience, worry, typical phrase or constraint that real students report; the claim and source IDs at the end of each line point to `research/part2-claims.md` and `research/sources.md`. The sources describe real students elsewhere, not students of the scenario university. Phrases in quotes are close paraphrases or translations for voice, not quotations to cite.

## Why they stop taking part

- Life outside the course is the main driver: work, family, health and personal situation make up most dropout reasons, while course design and administration are a small share (C2-011, C2-012, S204; C2-038, S209).
- Works long part-time hours, in one case 8–11 hours a day, to pay living costs and help parents with fees; arrives tired, dozes in class, results slip (C2-017, S202; C2-118, C2-119, C2-120, S218; C2-026, S205).
- Family and caring duties stop regular attendance: a new baby, children without daycare, a sick relative (C2-023, C2-025, S205).
- Mental or physical health problems (stress, depression, illness) come first among withdrawal reasons in one survey (C2-015, S202; C2-028, S205).
- Falls behind after one disruption (an illness, a job change), then decides it is "too late to catch up" and withdraws rather than fail (C2-025, C2-026, S205).
- Finds the online or asynchronous format hard: no fixed rhythm, home distractions, lectures boring on screen, motivation drains until it "doesn't feel like school" (C2-027, S205; C2-031, C2-032, S206).
- Struggles with unclear course pages, outdated syllabi, shifting due dates and slow grading; feels they are "teaching myself" (C2-016, C2-018, S202).
- Blames themselves rather than the format: "maybe I'm not cut out for university" (C2-034, S206).
- Feels anonymous in big courses, "just a number" or "just an account on the screen" (C2-001, S200; C2-070, S215).
- Feels isolated when studying off campus and has no idea how others are doing (C2-008, S200).

## Help-seeking and support

- Rarely asks for help early: embarrassed, overwhelmed, unsure the problem is serious enough or whom to contact (C2-021, S202).
- Most had not used academic support before withdrawing (C2-024, S205).
- Wants practical flexibility more than encouragement: simple extensions, moving deadlines, pausing and resuming without penalty (C2-013, C2-014, S204).
- Values a predictable weekly rhythm in online courses (posts Monday, replies Wednesday, quiz Friday) because it fits around work (C2-033, S206).
- Is annoyed by lecturers who never answer emails; remembers a lecturer who suggested a later short session as the one who helped (C2-029, C2-030, S205).

## Reactions to being contacted or flagged (contrasting voices)

- Welcomes it: a personal message from the tutor saying "I see you're struggling, come and see me" would be "a good thing" (C2-002, S200).
- Prefers neutral automated emails that list support options, so no one gets special treatment (C2-003, S200).
- Fears it: a "you are at risk" message could make them feel dejected and give up the course entirely (C2-004, S200).
- Finds it intrusive: repeated messages feel like the lecturer is "constantly watching"; "we're adults, not here to be babied" (C2-005, C2-006, S200).
- Doubts the data: logging in to the LMS does not mean they studied, and LMS time does not show real learning (C2-007, S200).

## Specific to Vietnam

- In Vietnamese online and distance programmes, dropouts point first to academic advising and student support, then to the programme and lecturers and technology (C2-035, C2-036, S208).
- Distance learning carries a social stigma ("not a real degree"), which weakens commitment (C2-037, S208).
- External reasons (no time, health, family, distance, fees) far outweigh internal ones (C2-038, C2-039, S209).
- Logs in to avoid an exam ban for too many absences, then turns off camera and microphone and keeps working or doing other things (C2-116, C2-115, S217; C2-058, S213).
- Stays silent with the camera off for fear of looking incompetent in front of the lecturer and classmates (losing face), and to protect privacy in a shared family room with old devices and weak internet (C2-062, C2-063, S213; C2-064, C2-066, S214).
- Turning off the camera is often due to cognitive load, not disengagement (C2-065, S214).
- Rural students hunt for wifi or phone signal to join class (C2-117, S217).
- Works part-time to cover living costs and help parents with fees (C2-120, S218).
