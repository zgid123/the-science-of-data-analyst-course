## 1.2. Problem Statement, Scope and the Need for Analytics

Building on this background and on interviews with five simulated stakeholders (Section 2), the problem is that, in
the program's online and blended courses, students who stop taking part are noticed late and patchily. Lecturers
notice some of them around week 5 or 6 (CL5); the program sees pass and withdrawal counts only after the semester
(PM1); and the student support office meets most of them on the academic warning list (AD1). Stakeholders place the
start of disengagement in the first weeks, as published studies also find [7], [8], and estimate that staff can
follow up only a few dozen students a week (AD3, CL10); the data audit of Milestone 2 will check both points. Staff
also lack shared, timely and trusted information on whom to contact first and why.

The project covers students enrolled after the add-drop period in the program's online and blended courses, one
semester at a time, and the decisions staff can take within it: whom to contact, when, by whom and with which offer.
Because institutions define retention differently, the measures are fixed first [5]: course completion means passing
the course, and withdrawal follows Circular 83/2026 [4]. Assessment rules, admissions, financial aid, the evaluation
of lecturers and any automated decision about a student are outside the scope.

Analytics is needed because the information to act earlier exists but is unused: early-warning work draws on student
records, continuous assessment and LMS activity that universities already hold [8], and the new rules require
student-level status records [4]. Analytics can turn them into a short, timely list with reasons and into course
patterns for the program; because data never give a complete picture of a student [11], the results support staff
judgement rather than replace it.
