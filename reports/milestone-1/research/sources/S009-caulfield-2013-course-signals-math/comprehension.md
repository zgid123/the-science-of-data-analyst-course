# S009 – Why the Course Signals math does not add up (Caulfield, Hapgood blog, 2013)

## Summary in my own words

Mike Caulfield responds to a 2013 Purdue press release claiming that taking two Course Signals courses raises graduation rates by 21%. He argues that the comparison is confounded by course load: students who stay enrolled and take more courses are mechanically more likely to have taken one or more Course Signals courses, so "number of CS courses" partly measures persistence itself. He shows with a simple probability model that a full-load student is likely to be tagged as having two or more Course Signals courses, and notes that the early one-course retention advantage disappeared as adoption grew, as his model predicts. He does not claim Course Signals has no effect, but says the cited increases are uninterpretable until the analysis controls for course load and first-semester dropouts. Comments and pingbacks show the critique was taken up by Feldstein M. (e-Literate), Essa A. and Clow D.

## Key facts

- The claim criticised – "it claims taking *two* classes using Course Signals boosts graduation rates by 21%"
- Mechanism – "each class you take is like an additional coin flip"
- Course load tagging – "just by taking a full load of classes and not dropping out first semester you’re likely to be tagged as a CS 2+ student"
- Early retention figures – "The one year retention rate is 97% for the one-CS course student, compared to just 83% for the no-CS student."
- Effect disappears – "Two years later, that retention effect has disappeared entirely"
- Confounding – "The effect disappears because the confounding variable is no longer correlated with the dependent variable."
- Needed control – "It would be trivially easy to account for these things — for example, by removing first semester dropouts from the equation, and only looking at students under full course load."
- Hedged conclusion – "So, do I believe that Course Signals works? Maybe, it’s possible."
- Author's self-description – "I’m a Literature and Linguistics M.A. who works in educational technology, not a statistician."
- Arithmetic slip acknowledged in comments – "Thanks Ted, I noticed the typo myself just after posting."
- Uptake – "Digging into the Purdue Course Signals Results |e-Literate" and "Looking harder at Course Signals | Doug Clow's Imaginatively-Titled Blog"

## Reliability

Secondary (blog commentary), used because it is the widely cited critique of the Course Signals retention claim; author Caulfield M., then at Washington State University Vancouver, writing in a personal capacity and not a statistician by his own account; not peer-reviewed. It contains an acknowledged arithmetic typo (16% should be 26%) that does not change the argument. Citation traction not measured (as of 2026-11-02).
