# S215 – Krause (2026), Ghosts in the (online teaching) machine

## Summary in my own words
An Eastern Michigan University writing professor with almost 40 years' experience blogs about a semester in which one first-year writing section went from 22 to 10 students, his highest withdrawal or fail rate. He teaches online asynchronously around discussion and small-group work, uses a low-stakes introduction post in week 1, and emails students who stop posting. When four students went silent in week 2, he asked their other instructors, who had noticed nothing because online gen-ed courses run like lecture halls where no one expects presence. They turned out to be fraudulent "ghost students". He reflects that online students are often treated as names in the Canvas gradebook, and that he might never have noticed had they stayed.

## Key facts
- Section attrition – "One of my sections of first-year writing started with 22 students and finished with 10, the highest withdrawal/fail rate I’ve ever had."
- Typical section – "My other section started with 25 and finished with 19, which is more typical"
- Week-1 low-stakes check – "we started with a simple post where students introduce themselves."
- Outreach – "I emailed each of them to tell them that they would need to participate in these discussions if they hoped to pass, and also they still had time to withdraw to receive a refund on their tuition."
- Colleagues did not notice – "All four of the instructors emailed me back and essentially said they hadn’t noticed anything unusual, and besides, this was only the second week of class."
- Lecture-style online courses – "no one really expects students in those classes to demonstrate a lot of presence."
- Students as gradebook names – "names that show up in the Canvas grade book with no other presence in the world."
- Quick dropping is normal – "It’s not that weird for students to sign up for an online class and then quickly stop participating because they decided it wasn’t a class for them."
