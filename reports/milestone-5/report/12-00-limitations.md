# 12. Limitations and Future Work

Section 11 answered AQ1 and AQ2 on the 2013 runs of OULAD and set out the expected outputs of AQ3 and AQ4. Every one of these answers holds within limits that come from the data, from how the KPI and the signs are measured, from the observational design and from the models still to be tested. We state these limits as conditions on the answers, because a limit that is known is part of the result: it tells the program manager, the lecturer-advisors and the university's leadership where an answer can be used and where it cannot. Throughout, we keep three situations apart. "No evidence found" means that a comparison was made on the data and showed no clear difference or association. "No data to test" means that the data needed for the comparison do not exist in OULAD or in any dataset screened in Milestone 2. "Not yet tested" means that the data exist but stay closed by design until the single AQ3 test, so that the test remains independent.

**Generalisability.** OULAD is an open stand-in for the university's own records (Change Log C01, OULAD as stand-in): every answer describes UK distance-learning modules of 2013–2014 and must be re-checked on the university's data before it informs D1, D2 or D3. The training data are narrow as well: 9 runs from 6 modules, with module CCC seen only in 2014, and each module has at most one earlier run of the same start month. A module run also lasts 234 to 269 days, longer than a university semester, so a week of a run and a week of a semester are not the same point in a course (C02, semester length as a stated limit). In addition, published work on predictive learning analytics warns against one model for all courses, because instructional conditions change what predicts success [S908]. As a result, the AQ1 rates and the AQ2 associations are reliable for the OULAD modules of 2013; the university's online and blended courses need their own recount before any rate or sign is used there.

**Measurement.** The primary KPI on OULAD is a proxy. Completion of a module run stands for completion of a course, and approved leave or transfer cannot be told apart from withdrawal, although Circular 83/2026 excludes students on approved leave and those who transfer from the withdrawal count [S116]. Uncertain records matter little: over all 22 runs, Milestone 3 found completion at day 14 of 54.9% instead of 54.7% without the 138 records with an unknown withdrawal or registration day, and in 2013 removing the 8 such records from the day-14 population leaves non-completion at 45.6% instead of 45.7%. The denominator matters much more, since it moves a run's rate by up to 9.9 points (Section 11.2.2), so a completion rate is comparable only under the same add-drop rule. Capacity is assumed rather than observed: the three to five contacts a week of each lecturer-advisor become lists of 3 or 5 per group of 20 students, and the 30–40 contacts a week of the support office become one list of 30 or 40 per run at a review point; both are scenarios, not plans, and runs stand in for the groups of each lecturer-advisor because OULAD records none (risk R8 of the Milestone 4 risk register). The frozen plan evaluates the lists at 15% and 25% of a run only, so a real caseload outside that range would need its own check. Finally, clicks measure activity on the platform, not learning, and the cut of the relative-activity sign at the run median is our choice; the sensitivity run at half the median gives 14.7 instead of 13.4 points and leaves the verdict unchanged (Section 11.3.4).

**Observational and causal limits.** The AQ2 estimates are associations within module runs, not effects. A student who stops clicking may already have decided to leave for reasons OULAD does not record, so removing the sign would not necessarily change the outcome. The same limit applies with more force to contact: confounding is especially serious when an active intervention is compared with no intervention [S904], which is exactly the comparison between contacted and non-contacted students, and OULAD records no contact at all. Nothing can therefore be said about the effect of contacting a student, and a ranking by risk is not a ranking by benefit, because the students at highest risk need not be those whom contact helps most. The SMART Goal measures progress in the pilot courses, not the effect of contact; attributing a change to the contacts needs a comparison design.

**Model and analysis limits.** No predictive model has been trained, by our choice of scope (Level 2 is optional, and the single-use 2014 test is kept for one complete run of the frozen protocol, Section 10), so the AQ3 and AQ4 answers are designs, and fold results from the 2013 development would serve only the choice between candidates. Within the AQ2 analysis, the binary activity signs miss part of the activity gradient: residuals lie outside ±2 standard errors in all five activity levels (Section 11.3.4), which describes the data better with a graded measure than with one threshold; with a continuous activity adjustment the misfit disappears and the click signs shrink, the below-median sign to 5.8 points (3.4–8.3), close to the five-point threshold (Section 11.3.4). The five-point criterion is lenient at a base rate near 44%, so the effect sizes rather than the verdict distinguish the signs; the intervals treat the 9 runs as fixed, so they understate how far a sign's association may vary in a later run; and the associations are estimated for the joint target, so whether a sign relates differently to failing and to withdrawing, which matters in modules such as GGG where most non-completers fail, is not estimated. Gate S1 of AQ3 sets no minimum size of gain, so a pass would be read with the number of extra non-completers listed. The fairness audit has its own limits. Calibration and balance across groups cannot all be satisfied at once unless prediction is perfect or base rates are equal [S906], and base rates do differ by deprivation band and disability (Section 11.2.2), so the project fixes one primary criterion, equal recall at capacity, and accepts that the others may not hold. A published fairness study describes students with a declared disability as strongly under-represented in OULAD [S907]; they form 3,164 of the 32,593 enrolments, about one in ten, enough for a pooled confirmatory check on 1,488 test enrolments at day 27 but too few for rates run by run, and a confirmatory group without a flag of rule FR1 shows no evidence of a gap at that precision, not that the list is fair; students aged 55 or over are too few for any confirmatory check.

**Ethical limits.** A risk flag can stereotype students, and predictive analysis in learning analytics carries a potential for bias and stereotyping [S905]. Student identity is transient and bound to its context [S905], so a flag must not become a fixed label; for this reason a flag in the proposed use leads to support and expires with the run, and the students it names can prove the prediction wrong. Learning analytics is also a moral practice in which students are participatory agents [S905], which is why their acceptance of activity-based contact has to be confirmed before the pilot (Section 12.1). The Milestone 4 risk register holds sixteen risks, each with its control, check and residual risk.
