## Appendix XV – Limitations and Future Work in Detail

This appendix supports Section 12. It discusses each kind of limit, gives the limitation log with the effect of each limit on the conclusions, and sets out the future work in order with the data each step needs, the legal duties of use at the university and the design of the pilot.

Section 11 answered AQ1 and AQ2 on the 2013 and 2014 runs of OULAD and set out the expected outputs of AQ3 and AQ4. Every one of these answers holds within limits that come from the data, from how the KPI and the signs are measured, from the observational design and from the models still to be tested. We state these limits as conditions on the answers, because a limit that is known is part of the result: it tells the program manager, the lecturer-advisors and the university's leadership where an answer can be used and where it cannot. Throughout, we keep three situations apart. "No evidence found" means that a comparison was made on the data and showed no clear difference or association. "No data to test" means that the data needed for the comparison do not exist in OULAD or in any dataset screened in Milestone 2. "Not yet tested" means that the data exist but the analysis waits for the single AQ3 test, so that the test remains independent.

**Generalisability.** OULAD is an open stand-in for the university's own records (Change Log C01, OULAD as stand-in): every answer describes UK distance-learning modules of 2013–2014 and must be re-checked on the university's data before it informs D1, D2 or D3. The training data are narrow as well: 9 runs from 6 modules, with module CCC seen only in 2014, and each module has at most one earlier run of the same start month. A module run also lasts 234 to 269 days, longer than a university semester, so a week of a run and a week of a semester are not the same point in a course (C02, semester length as a stated limit). In addition, published work on predictive learning analytics warns against one model for all courses, because instructional conditions change what predicts success [15]. As a result, the AQ1 rates and the AQ2 associations are reliable for the OULAD modules of 2013–2014, where the two years agree; the university's online and blended courses need their own recount before any rate or sign is used there.

**Measurement.** The primary KPI on OULAD is a proxy. Completion of a module run stands for completion of a course, and approved leave or transfer cannot be told apart from withdrawal, although Circular 83/2026 excludes students on approved leave and those who transfer from the withdrawal count [2]. Uncertain records matter little: over all 22 runs, Milestone 3 found completion at day 14 of 54.9% instead of 54.7% without the 138 records with an unknown withdrawal or registration day, and in 2013 removing the 8 such records from the day-14 population leaves non-completion at 45.6% instead of 45.7%, and in 2014 removing 91 leaves 44.6% instead of 44.9%. The denominator matters much more, since it moves a run's rate by up to 13.6 points (Appendix XII), so a completion rate is comparable only under the same add-drop rule. Capacity is assumed rather than observed: the three to five contacts a week of each lecturer-advisor become lists of 3 or 5 per group of 20 students, and the 30–40 contacts a week of the support office become one list of 30 or 40 per run at a review point; both are scenarios, not plans, and runs stand in for the groups of each lecturer-advisor because OULAD records none (risk R8 of the Milestone 4 risk register). The frozen plan evaluates the lists at 15% and 25% of a run only, so a real caseload outside that range would need its own check. Finally, clicks measure activity on the platform, not learning, and the cut of the relative-activity sign at the run median is our choice; the sensitivity run at half the median gives 14.7 instead of 13.4 points and leaves the verdict unchanged (Appendix XIII).

**Observational and causal limits.** The AQ2 estimates are associations within module runs, not effects. One possibility the data cannot rule out is that a student who stops clicking has already decided to leave for reasons OULAD does not record; in that case, removing the sign would not change the outcome. The same limit applies with more force to contact: confounding is especially serious when an active intervention is compared with no intervention [14], which is exactly the comparison between contacted and non-contacted students, and OULAD records no contact at all. Nothing can therefore be said about the effect of contacting a student, and a ranking by risk is not a ranking by benefit, because the students at highest risk need not be those whom contact helps most. The SMART Goal measures progress in the pilot courses, not the effect of contact; attributing a change to the contacts needs a comparison design.

**Model and analysis limits.** No predictive model has been trained, by our choice of scope (Level 2 is optional, and the single-use 2014 test is kept for one complete run of the frozen protocol, Section 10), so the AQ3 and AQ4 answers are designs, and fold results from the 2013 development would serve only the choice between candidates. Within the AQ2 analysis, the binary activity signs miss part of the activity gradient: residuals lie outside ±2 standard errors in all five activity levels (Appendix XIII), which describes the data better with a graded measure than with one threshold; with a continuous activity adjustment the misfit disappears in 2013, and shrinks without disappearing in 2014, and the click signs shrink, the below-median sign to 5.8 points (3.4–8.3) in 2013, close to the five-point threshold, and to 5.9 points (3.7–8.1) in 2014, where it keeps its direction in only 4 of 7 modules and no longer meets the criterion (Appendix XIII). The five-point criterion is lenient at a base rate near 44%, so the effect sizes rather than the verdict distinguish the signs; the intervals treat the runs as fixed, so they understate how far a sign's association may vary in a later run; and the associations are estimated for the joint target, so whether a sign relates differently to failing and to withdrawing, which matters in modules such as GGG where most non-completers fail, is not estimated. The AQ3 test on 2014 stays independent only while the design Milestone 4 fixed on 2013 is kept, because AQ1 and AQ2 have now described the 2014 outcomes; a change to a predictor, a candidate, the selection rule or the gate would need a test population not yet seen (C15). Gate S1 of AQ3 sets no minimum size of gain, so a pass would be read with the number of extra non-completers listed. The fairness audit has its own limits. Calibration and balance across groups cannot all be satisfied at once unless prediction is perfect or base rates are equal [6], and base rates do differ by deprivation band and disability (Appendix XII), so the project fixes one primary criterion, equal recall at capacity, and accepts that the others may not hold. A published fairness study describes students with a declared disability as strongly under-represented in OULAD [7]; they form 3,164 of the 32,593 enrolments, about one in ten, enough for a pooled confirmatory check on 1,488 test enrolments at day 27 but too few for rates run by run, and a confirmatory group without a flag of rule FR1 shows no evidence of a gap at that precision, not that the list is fair; students aged 55 or over are too few for any confirmatory check.

**Ethical limits.** A risk flag can stereotype students, and predictive analysis in learning analytics carries a potential for bias and stereotyping [16]. Student identity is transient and bound to its context [16], so a flag must not become a fixed label; for this reason a flag in the proposed use leads to support and expires with the run, and the students it names can prove the prediction wrong. Learning analytics is also a moral practice in which students are participatory agents [16], which is why their acceptance of activity-based contact has to be confirmed before the pilot (Section 12.1). The Milestone 4 risk register holds sixteen risks, each with its control, check and residual risk.

The first two steps can run on OULAD as it stands. The Level 2 prototype of AQ3 follows the protocol Milestone 4 froze: baselines first, the penalised logistic regression and its challengers on leave-one-run-out folds of 2013, a second freeze with the test access recorded, and a single scoring of the 13 runs of 2014, with unfavourable results reported as they come. The design of that test stays as Milestone 4 fixed it, because AQ1 and AQ2 have already described the 2014 runs (C15). The AQ4 list and its fairness audit follow the test, and their outputs are already specified, so no choice remains to be made once the AQ3 scores exist.

The remaining steps need data that no open dataset holds. None of the seven candidate datasets screened in Milestone 2 records contacts, attendance or year of study for the same students, so three parts of the proposal can only be answered with the pilot's own records: which contact works best and what its effect is (AQ4's deferred part), comparisons by year of study, delivery mode and attendance (AQ1 and AQ2), and the follow-up rate of secondary KPI 2 together with the contact part of secondary KPI 1. We do not impute or simulate any of these variables, because invented values would turn assumptions into apparent evidence. Several design values also rest on assumptions or on simulated stakeholder views and must be replaced by the university's records: the baseline completion rate, the capacity figures, the one-week data delay, the list-length scenarios of AQ4 and the ten-point fairness gap of rule FR1. The acceptance of activity-based contact, in turn, was voiced only by simulated students in Milestone 1, so it needs confirmation from real students in Vietnam before the pilot.

Use at the university also brings legal duties. Vietnam's Law on Artificial Intelligence, No. 134/2025/QH15, applies from 1 March 2026 and requires that human control and the ability to intervene be kept over every decision of an AI system [26], and Decision 33/2026/QĐ-TTg lists among the high-risk uses in education AI systems that automatically assess or rank learners and those that monitor or analyse learner behaviour [27], so a contact list built from a model is likely to be classed as high-risk. A pilot therefore needs a risk classification first, and staff keep the final say on every contact. The pilot itself is proposed as a randomised comparison with time zero at the eligibility cut-off, because only random assignment separates the effect of contact from the reasons students are flagged [14]. Randomisation would take place among the flagged students within the pilot courses and answers whether contact helps, while the course-level comparison of Section 2 measures the SMART Goal; the pilot protocol fixes, before contact begins, the share of flagged students assigned to contact, which also bounds how much of the five-point target the contacts can carry. Steps 3 and 4 must be complete before contact begins in Semester 2 of 2026–2027, which is why the start date of Section 2 is conditional on them. Its contacts are logged with their outcomes before any model is retrained, since once the list is used, outcomes partly reflect the contacts made and a model retrained on them would learn its own influence.

Finally, a working model is not enough for adoption. At the Open University, the spread of predictive learning analytics depended on the faculty's engagement, on teachers acting as champions, on evidence generation and dissemination, on digital literacy and on conceptions about teaching online [24]. We therefore propose that the lecturer-advisors and the support office take part in the pilot as its users from the start, and that its evidence, including a negative result, be reported back to them. Section 13 turns from the data to the way generative AI supported this work and how its output was verified.
