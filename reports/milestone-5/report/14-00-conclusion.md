# 14. Conclusion

The project set out to improve successful course completion in the program's online and blended courses by identifying students who stop taking part early enough to support them within the semester, with a SMART Goal of five more percentage points of completion in the pilot courses by the end of 2027–2028 (Section 2). From this goal came four final questions: how many enrolments end without completion and when non-completers first miss a task (AQ1, descriptive), which early signs go with non-completion (AQ2, diagnostic), how well non-completion can be estimated at the end of weeks 2 and 4 against a simple rule (AQ3, predictive), and whom a list of limited capacity should name first (AQ4, prescriptive ranking), each tied to the contact, support-time and extension decisions D1–D3 (Sections 3 and 11.1).

The data could support part of this ambition. No open dataset of Vietnamese courses exists, so the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset stands in for the university's records, with a dated final result, dated tasks and daily platform activity for every enrolment (Sections 4 and 5). It holds no delivery mode, year of study, attendance, staff capacity or contact record, so the comparisons by delivery mode and year of study, the attendance sign and the choice of contact have no data to test (Sections 5.1 and 12). The prepared version of the data, with every sign dated on or before its cut-off and the 2014 runs kept apart as a test set, makes the remaining questions answerable without leakage (Section 7).

The appropriate analysis differs by question, and its weight differs accordingly. For AQ1, grouped rates with intervals under a fixed denominator answer the question directly, and on the 9 runs of 2013 they show that 45.7% (44.8–46.6) of the 12,076 enrolments still registered at day 14 did not complete, with rates from 24.9% to 57.5% between runs and a rate that can move by up to 9.9 points with the denominator alone (Section 11.2). For AQ2, a within-run logistic regression read as an association shows that all five early signs go with later non-completion, a missed task by 40.8 points (37.7–43.9) after adjustment for the others, in the same direction across modules and under every sensitivity run, including a continuous adjustment for activity that shrinks the click signs (Section 11.3). For AQ3 and AQ4, a penalised logistic model judged once against the early-sign rule on the 2014 runs, and a capacity-limited list audited for fairness, are the appropriate methods, and their outputs remain expected rather than measured (Section 11.4).

What can be concluded is therefore bounded by the level of each answer. Non-completion in the OULAD modules of 2013 was common, varied strongly between runs and start months, and showed in missed tasks mostly after week 4, so an early list cannot rest on missed tasks alone; the inactivity signs that the early-sign rule adds go with later non-completion beyond what missed tasks show (Section 11.3), and how many later non-completers the rule's list reaches is measured with the rule baseline of the AQ3 test. The early signs identify students with clearly higher rates of non-completion within module runs and can serve as reasons that staff check beside each name. What cannot be concluded is that these signs cause non-completion, that a model ranks students better than the rule, or that contacting a listed student raises completion: a regression capturing associations is descriptive rather than causal [S900], questions of effectiveness need a randomised experiment [S904], and even the Open University's own evaluation of predictive learning analytics warns against a causal reading [S112]. Every rate and association describes UK distance-learning modules of 2013–2014 and must be recounted on the university's own records before it guides a decision (Section 12).

The next step follows from this boundary. The predictive test of AQ3 runs once on the 2014 runs under the protocol frozen in Milestone 4, followed by the AQ4 list with its fairness audit and the 2014 replication of AQ2 (Section 12.1). At the university, the baseline completion rate is recounted under the add-drop rule, the list is classified under Vietnam's rules on high-risk AI use, and a randomised pilot with contacts and outcomes logged answers the question that no open dataset can: whether, and for whom, early contact improves completion (Sections 11.5 and 12.1). The early-sign rule is defined and interpretable, its parts go with later non-completion in 2013, and its list performance is measured as a baseline of the AQ3 test before the pilot relies on it; the signs as reasons and the AQ1 measure of completion are ready to be checked on the university's records and used in the pilot.
