## Appendix VIII – Modeling Briefs and Method Rationale

This appendix supports Section 8. It gives the readiness of each question at entry, the development data, the Modeling Briefs, the technical tasks, the eight selection criteria and the rationale for the baseline, candidates and primary proposal of each question, including the specification with which AQ2 was run.

**Readiness at entry.** The questions entered the modeling stage in the state Milestone 3 rated them. AQ1 was ready on the enrolment and assessed-task views; AQ2 was ready for association only on the day-27 feature view; AQ3 was ready, with the day-13 list resting on activity alone because almost no task falls due by day 13; and AQ4 was ready as a ranking, with the choice of contact deferred to a pilot. The six entry conditions of the course for modeling held for AQ1 to AQ3 and for the ranking part of AQ4: prepared views with clear traceability, a stable target and features, defined leakage controls and split, quality issues handled or kept on purpose, no synthetic data as evidence, and data that rebuild from the raw files. Milestone 4 then checked its own entry gate of five conditions, approved questions, prepared dataset, leakage controls, known limitations and traceability, and all five held, so the questions could be modelled as they stood, with the contact part of AQ4 left deferred.

**Why a ranking is needed.** The early-sign rule (C10) already names students at risk without any model: it flags 20.2% of enrolled students at day 13 and 28.8% at day 27 over the 22 runs. Staff capacity, assumed at 3 or 5 contacts per group of 20 students (a scenario value, Section 2.1), covers 15% or 25% of a run, so in many runs the rule names more students than a list can hold, and staff need an order of whom to contact first. The rule alone cannot set that order, which is the task of AQ3 and AQ4. A prescriptive answer in the full sense, which contact to make and with what effect, is out of reach, because the data hold no action, no cost and no outcome after a contact; the only prescriptive output OULAD supports is a list of a given size under an assumed capacity.

**Development data.** All model development uses the 9 runs of 2013. At day 27 they hold 11,811 enrolments, of whom 5,245 (44.4%) did not complete, against 15,724 enrolments in the 13 test runs of 2014; at day 13 the figures are 12,080 enrolments with 5,519 non-completers, against 15,989 test enrolments. No test outcome enters the choice of the predictive model: the predictors, candidate configurations, selection rule and gate were fixed in Milestone 4 on the 2013 runs alone (C14). AQ1 and AQ2 describe the 2014 runs as well, AQ1 on both years and the 22 runs pooled and AQ2 as a replication on 2014, without changing that design (C15). The 22-run figures, such as the 28,061 enrolments of the KPI population and their 54.7% completion, are recomputed from the prepared data and equal those Milestone 3 reported.

**Purpose first.** Before any algorithm is named, each question's purpose fixes how its result may be read. Explanatory modeling tests causal explanations, whereas predictive modeling aims at predicting new or future observations, and rankings count as predictions [10]. A regression used only to capture associations, with neither a causal nor a predictive aim, is descriptive in the same framework [10]. AQ2 therefore keeps the diagnostic level with association as its technical task and is never read as cause, while AQ3 and AQ4 are predictions and must be judged on data the model has not seen, because explanatory power does not imply predictive power [10].

**Modeling Briefs.** For each question, a Modeling Brief records eight attributes before any algorithm is named: the question and its wording, its analytics level, the decision it supports, the unit of analysis, the outcome or target, the information allowed as input with its time window, the expected output and the main constraint. Naming the expected output matters, because the same binary outcome can be reported as classes, as probabilities or as a ranking, and each form needs its own measure of quality. **Technical tasks.** The briefs translate into four technical tasks. AQ1 is aggregation: rates and shares grouped by module, run, start month and year, each with its denominator and an interval. AQ2 is association within module run: how much more often students with a sign by day 27 fail to complete than comparable students of the same run without it. AQ3 is binary classification at two prediction points, the ends of day 13 and day 27, trained on earlier runs and tested on a later one, as the model would be used on a run still in progress. AQ4 is ranking under a fixed capacity, where the quality of the top of the list, the part on which staff act, matters more than the predicted values themselves. AQ3 and AQ4 share one target and one population, so AQ4 reuses the AQ3 scores and adds only the rule that cuts the ranking at the length capacity allows. Section 8.1 sets the baselines and candidate methods for each task and the primary proposal with its rationale.

**Criteria.** Each candidate is judged on the eight criteria that the course makes compulsory: fit to the question, whether the method directly produces the output the question needs; fit to the data, whether sample size, feature types, sparsity, imbalance, time structure and granularity suit it; assumptions, what the method requires of the data and whether the data roughly meet it; interpretability, how much of the logic the users need to understand; complexity against the baseline, what a more complex method is expected to add; robustness and reproducibility, whether it can be evaluated stably, rerun and kept free of leakage; ethics and fairness, whether its errors could harm a group of students and whether it uses a sensitive attribute; and actionability, whether its output supports the decision it serves. Four findings from the literature on prediction systems, summarised in Section 3.1 of the Milestone 4 report, settle most ties: a simple heuristic often delivers much of the gain, so predictive work starts from a rule baseline and a simple initial model; when many models reach about the same error, the simpler one can be chosen without loss; complexity is costly to maintain over time; and for high-stakes decisions about people, an inherently interpretable model is preferable to a black box explained afterwards.

**AQ1: grouped rates.** AQ1 is descriptive, so it is answered with descriptive statistics rather than machine learning. The baseline is the pooled rate, set beside a second denominator, the rate over all enrolments rather than only those registered at day 14, so that the comparison shows which rates depend on the population definition, and the rate without the enrolments flagged for an unknown withdrawal or registration day. The primary method is a table of grouped rates: non-completion and the four outcome shares by module, run and start month, each with its events, its denominator and a 95% Wilson score interval, and the cumulative share of enrolments with a first missed TMA or CMA by week, compared by outcome. The difference between years is computed as the 2014 rate minus the 2013 rate with a 95% Newcombe hybrid score interval, under both denominators (Change Log C15). On the 9 runs of 2013 the two denominators are 12,076 enrolments registered at day 14 and all 13,529 enrolments, and on the 13 runs of 2014 they are 15,985 and 19,064.

**AQ2: association within module run.** The baseline is the unadjusted rate difference per sign, the gap in non-completion between students with and without the sign. The primary approach is a logistic regression of non-completion on the signs with a fixed effect per module run, adjustment for previous attempts and credits studied, and sign × module terms, so that each sign's association is estimated within the run and may differ by module. Because every run of the modules is observed rather than sampled, the model uses robust standard errors without clustering; the intervals therefore describe these 9 runs, and the variation that matters when the signs are applied to later runs shows in the module estimates (Appendix XIII) rather than in the intervals. The mixed-effects alternative is set aside, because 6 modules and 9 training runs are too few to estimate the variance between them. The effect is reported as the average risk difference in percentage points, with the odds ratio beside it, because at a non-completion rate near 44% odds ratios read larger than the corresponding risk ratios. The signs are correlated, so their mutually adjusted estimates are partial effects, smaller than each sign's own gap; both are therefore shown side by side. The smallest training run holds 369 enrolments with 91 non-completers.

In Milestone 5 we ran this specification on the 11,811 enrolments registered at day 27 in the 9 runs of 2013, and five choices that the plan left open were fixed before the model was fitted. The signs are five: a task due by day 27 missed, a TMA submitted late, no click on days 21–27, clicks on days 0–27 below the median of the same run, and no click before day 0; the last one is named among the inputs of the AQ2 Modeling Brief of Milestone 4, beside the four kinds of sign in the question's wording, which stays as Milestone 4 decided it. The relative-activity sign uses the literal reading "below the run median", with "below half the median" as a sensitivity run. Previous attempts and credits (per 30 credits) enter linearly. The robust covariance is the heteroskedasticity-consistent form HC1. Sign × module terms are coded as deviations from the average module, so that the reported odds ratio is the one at the average module, with the module values beside it. The task-based signs are defined in the 7 runs with a task due by day 27 and in 4 modules, the click-based signs in all 9 runs and 6 modules, and the model has 37 parameters. Each sign appears in three columns: unadjusted, adjusted for run, background and its own module terms, and mutually adjusted for all signs. Section 11.3 reports the results.

**AQ3: proposed predictive model.** Three baselines are proposed, because a model is useful only if it beats what staff could do without it: the early-sign rule as frozen in C10, ranked by its number of signs with ties broken by lower relative activity; a background-only logistic model that uses module, start month, previous attempts, credits, education and registration lead but no activity; and a random list. The primary proposal is a penalised logistic regression on the 19 primary predictors, one model per cut-off, with 23 parameters once module and start month are coded. Counts enter as the logarithm of one plus the count, the registration lead is median-filled with a missing indicator, and every predictor is standardised, with each of these transforms refitted inside each development fold. The challenger is gradient-boosted trees, which read the predictors untransformed and may capture interactions the linear model misses; they, or the extended predictor set, replace the primary approach only if they beat it by the margin fixed in advance (Section 9.1). A discrete-time survival model stays an extension, because decision D1 acts at two fixed cut-offs and needs no timing, and the target joins Fail, decided only at the end of the run, with Withdrawn.

The rationale for the primary proposal follows the criteria. Staff must see why a student is listed, and a logistic model gives each student's reasons from the same coefficients that rank the student. The data would support it amply: about 230 non-completers per parameter, and 11,811 training enrolments at day 27, about seven times the 1,731 that a published minimum-sample-size criterion for prediction models requires for this setting (Milestone 4). The classes are close in size, so no resampling is needed, and calibration would be checked rather than assumed.

**AQ4: proposed ranking.** The baselines are a random list, whose expected recall equals k/n for a list of length k in a run of n enrolments, and the rule-ranked list of the same length. The primary proposal is a top-k list per run from the AQ3 scores, where k follows the assumed capacity of 3 or 5 contacts per group of 20 students (a scenario value, Section 2.1), that is 15% or 25% of the enrolments registered at the cut-off, with one list per run. The alternative, risk tiers set from capacity, would group students without an order inside each tier and is kept as a candidate only. The list would give staff ranks and each student's main contributing signs, and probabilities only when calibration holds; the member of staff decides whom to contact. Ranking students by the expected benefit of a contact is rejected, because it needs randomised or otherwise unconfounded data on contacts and their outcomes, which OULAD does not hold. The assumption that the order of risk matches the order of need therefore stays untested until a randomised pilot.

**Traceability of the choices.** Each choice traces back to its question: AQ1 to grouped rates with intervals, AQ2 to a logistic regression with run fixed effects, AQ3 to a penalised logistic regression judged by normalised recall at capacity, and AQ4 to a top-k list per run with reasons. The method changes only how a question is measured where a Change Log entry records it, namely C14 for AQ2 and C15 for AQ1, both of which protect the independence of the AQ3 test. Section 9 sets how each of these methods would be judged good enough.
