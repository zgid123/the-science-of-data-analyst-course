## Appendix II – Goal, Indicators and Decisions

This appendix supports Section 2. It gives the pilot design behind the SMART Goal with its check against the five SMART criteria, the full definition of each KPI, and the classification of the three decisions with the evidence each needs.

**Business Goal.** Our Business Goal is to **improve successful course completion in the program's online and blended
courses by identifying students who stop taking part early enough to support them within the semester**. The goal
names an outcome for the organisation, more students passing their courses, rather than an activity such as analysing
LMS data or a technical solution such as a prediction model; a model, a list or a dashboard is only a means towards
it. The second half of the goal states when the outcome must be pursued, within the semester, because the stakeholders
of Section 1.1 and the published studies of Section 1 both place the useful moment for support in the first weeks of
a course. The goal matters to students, for whom a failed course costs a semester's time and fees, and to the
university, whose withdrawal rate is tied to its enrolment quota.

The pilot courses are about four of the program's online and blended courses with about 600 enrolments in total,
both assumptions, chosen before the pilot begins in Semester 2 of 2026–2027, so that the target cannot be met by changing which courses are counted; the start date holds only if data access and the risk classification of Section 12.1 are in place by then. Their completion rate is compared with that of similar courses outside the pilot,
so that a good result cannot come simply from choosing easy courses. This course-level comparison measures the SMART Goal; whether contact itself helps is a separate question, which the randomised pilot of Section 12.1 answers among the flagged students of the pilot courses. The baseline is the completion rate of the same
courses in 2024–2025 and 2025–2026, recounted under Circular 83/2026 so that approved leave and transfers do not count
as non-completion. Table A.2 checks the goal against each of the five SMART criteria.

**Table A.2. SMART check of the goal**

| Criterion | How the goal meets it |
| :---- | :---- |
| Specific | One measure (course completion rate), one population (students enrolled after the add-drop period) and one set of courses (the pilot courses, fixed in advance) |
| Measurable | Completion and its denominator are read from the academic management system at the end of each semester, through the primary KPI of Section 2.1 |
| Achievable | At the assumed 70% baseline, about 180 of 600 enrolments end without a pass; five more passes per 100 enrolments means that about 30 of them, one in six, would have to pass after support. Reaching them fits the weekly capacity that Section 1.1 reports, but that one in six would then pass is an assumption, not an estimate: no data in the project measure the effect of contact, so the pilot tests it |
| Relevant | Measures the Business Goal directly and answers the program manager's and the support office's needs for real completion and earlier contact |
| Time-bound | Contact runs from Semester 2 of 2026–2027; the target is read on the pilot courses pooled over the 2027–2028 academic year |

**Why the target remains an assumption.** The target is demanding but achievable at the scale of the pilot. Because
the gains reported for early-warning systems are contested, as Milestone 1 noted, the five-point target remains an
assumption to be reviewed after the pilot, in the same way as the 70% baseline. The horizon of a full academic year
follows the validation round of Section 1.1, in which the program manager doubted that five points could be gained in one
semester and accepted the goal in this longer form.

**What the goal can and cannot show.** The SMART Goal measures progress, not the effect of contact. Students who are
contacted may have passed anyway, so a rise in completion shows that the situation has improved but not why; whether
contact itself raised completion needs a comparison design, which Section 12.1 places in the randomised pilot. The
Business Goal, the SMART Goal and the three decisions of Section 2.1 have stayed the same from Milestone 1 to the
final report: moving the analysis to an open stand-in dataset narrowed how the questions are measured, not what the
project aims for. Section 2.1 turns the goal into indicators that can be read during and after each semester.

**Primary KPI: course completion rate.** The number of enrolments in the pilot courses that end in a pass, divided by
the number of enrolments after the add-drop period, pooled over the pilot courses. A fail and a withdrawal from the
course both count as non-completion, while approved leave and transfers to another program leave the denominator, as
under Circular 83/2026. The KPI is the SMART Goal's own measure and is read at the end of each semester from the
academic management system. On OULAD the KPI has a proxy. The Open University's registration window closes two weeks
after a module run starts, so Milestone 2 mapped the add-drop period onto the first 14 days (C03), and the proxy population, under the population rule of Milestone 3 (C09), counts an enrolment when the student registered by day 14 (or the registration day is unknown) and had not unregistered by it.
This rule removes 58 late registrants from the 28,119 enrolments still registered on day 14 and
leaves 28,061 enrolments over the 22 runs; on the 9 runs of 2013 it gives the 12,076 enrolments on which Section 11
reports AQ1. The proxy cannot separate leave or transfer from withdrawal, because OULAD records neither.

**Secondary KPI 1: early-sign contact rate.** The number of students meeting the early-sign rule at the end of week 4, the data cut-off of the week-5 list (day 27 on OULAD), who are contacted by the end of week 5, divided by the number of students meeting the rule. The KPI shows whether students who begin to
disengage are reached while support can still help, and it can be read in week 6, within the semester. Students who
meet the rule but are not on a list because capacity is full count as not contacted, so a low value signals that
capacity is too small rather than that staff failed; the KPI is monitored without a target until the pilot shows what
is achievable. The early-sign rule (C10) flags a student who, by the data cut-off, has not submitted a task (a TMA
or CMA) that was due, has submitted a TMA late, or has
had no activity on the learning platform in the previous seven days. The rule began in Milestone 1 as "two or more
assessed tasks missed or late by the end of week 4"; on OULAD a rule on tasks alone cannot fire in 6 of the 22 runs at
day 27 or in 21 runs at day 13, because few tasks fall due so early, so Milestone 3 added the inactivity part, which can
fire in every run. One rule now serves the whole project, on OULAD and in the pilot (Change Log entry C17), so that the
KPI counts the same students as the rule that the AQ3 model must beat; the pilot checks the rule against the
university's own task calendar before use. On OULAD the rule flags 20.2% of the 28,069 enrolments registered at day 13 and 28.8% of the 27,535 registered at day 27 over the 22 runs. Lists are sized by capacity: the three to five contacts a week of each lecturer-advisor (Section 1.1) become 3 or 5 contacts per group of 20 students, a group size that Milestone 4 assumed because neither the scenario nor OULAD records how many students each lecturer-advisor follows, so a list of 15% or 25% of a run is a scenario value. The rule names more students than such a list can hold in many runs, which is why AQ3 and AQ4 rank
students rather than list everyone the rule flags. The sign part of the KPI can be computed on OULAD; its contact part
needs a contact record and is measured only in the pilot.

**Secondary KPI 2: follow-up rate.** The number of students on a contact list who are contacted within seven days of
the list being sent, divided by the number of students on the list. It shows whether the lists fit staff capacity and
are actually used, because a list that staff cannot follow up is worse than no list. It is read one week after each
review point. Both secondary KPIs depend on a contact record, a shared log of who contacted which student, when, how
and with what result; the university has no such record today, and no open dataset holds one, so both are measured
only in the pilot.

**Decisions supported.** The goal and its indicators serve three decisions, made by different people at different
levels of the university. We classify each by its level (strategic planning, management control or operational
control, following Anthony's levels), by whether it is programmed in Simon's sense, that is routine with a worked-out
procedure, and by whether it is a one-way door, nearly irreversible, or a two-way door, which can be reversed quickly.
Table A.3 gives the three decisions with their classification, the questions that serve them and the evidence each
needs.

**Table A.3. Decisions supported and the evidence they need**

| Decision | Who decides | Level; programmed?; door | Served by | Evidence needed |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| D1. Whom to contact at each review point, by whom and with which offer | Course lecturers, lecturer-advisors and the support office | Operational control; programmed, repeated at every review point; two-way, since a contact that does not help can be followed by another | AQ3 and AQ4 (the list and its order); AQ2 (the reason shown with each name) | A list that reaches more later non-completers than the early-sign rule on a list of the same length; each name checked by a member of staff |
| D2. Where to place the time of the support office and the lecturer-advisors each semester | Program manager | Management control; semi-programmed, with judgement about courses and staff; two-way, since staff can be moved again next semester | AQ1 and AQ2; primary KPI | A pattern repeated over several semesters, correct records and agreement with staff |
| D3. Whether to extend the approach to all courses and link data routinely | University leadership, legal staff and the data office, on the pilot results that the program manager brings | Strategic planning; non-programmed, with no earlier case; close to one-way, since it changes the university's data practice and students' trust | AQ1 and the KPIs for pilot and comparison courses | Pilot results, legal approval, and AQ1 and the KPIs measured before and after the pilot |

**How the level of a decision sets the evidence.** D1 is frequent and reversible, so it can start with the simple
early-sign rule checked by staff, and a model's ranking replaces the rule only if it reaches more later non-completers
on a list of the same length. D2 moves scarce staff time between courses, so it needs a pattern that repeats rather
than one difficult semester. D3 commits the university's data practice and the trust of its students, so it needs the
strongest evidence, including an honest account of what the pilot did not show. Even a negative pilot result has value:
it shows, at small scale and before any one-way commitment, which part of the path from description to action does not
work. Section 3 states the four questions, AQ1–AQ4, that supply this evidence.
