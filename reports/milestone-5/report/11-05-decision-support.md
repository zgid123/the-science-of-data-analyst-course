## 11.5. Answers by Question and Decision Support

Sections 11.2 to 11.4 answered each final question at the level of evidence it reached: AQ1 and AQ2 with real results on the 2013 runs, AQ3 and AQ4 with expected outputs and fixed criteria. The four answers are set here side by side with the evidence, method, confidence and implication of each, and are then turned into support for the three decisions of Section 2.1: whom to contact at each review point (D1), where to place support time each semester (D2) and whether to extend the approach to all courses (D3).

### 11.5.2. Why recommendations remain options to test

All four answers rest on observational data, and none of them can show what contact would achieve. Questions of effectiveness are ideally answered by a randomised experiment, and observational data can only try to emulate one [S904]; such an emulation fails when the data lack sufficient information on the baseline confounders that drive both the decision and the outcome [S904]. OULAD records no contact at all, so it cannot even supply the comparison between contacted and non-contacted students. Even the Open University's own evaluation of its predictive learning analytics, which did involve teacher action, warns that any causal interpretation of its findings should be treated with caution [S112]. For these reasons we present every prescriptive statement below as a decision option: a course of action the evidence makes reasonable, together with the hypothesis the pilot would test before the option becomes practice.

For D1, the evidence supports a clear candidate. The early-sign rule (C10) is defined and interpretable, it can be computed from records the university already holds, its flagged share is known from Milestone 3, each of its parts goes with later non-completion within the module runs of 2013, and its activity part covers the runs where no task has yet fallen due. How well a list of 3 or 5 per 20 built from it reaches later non-completers has not been measured: that is baseline B2 of the AQ3 test, measured there before D1 relies on the list, while the AQ3 model can replace the rule's order only by passing the single gate fixed in Milestone 4. What the list cannot yet support is the second half of D1, the choice of person and offer, because no data in OULAD record contacts; the option is to leave that choice open and randomise in the pilot, so that the pilot answers it.

For D2, the AQ1 rates show where non-completion concentrates in OULAD, and the AQ2 signs show where students stop taking part early. The option of placing early support time where both are highest is reasonable, but it depends on the same pattern appearing in the university's courses, since OULAD describes UK distance-learning modules of 2013–2014 rather than the program's online and blended courses. For D3, the project offers a measurement method rather than a verdict: a completion rate computed by one rule for pilot and comparison courses, a ranking gate fixed in advance and a fairness audit. An extension close to a one-way door should wait for a pilot result, even a negative one, because the pilot shows at small scale which part of the path works.

Taken together, the answers support preparing the pilot with the early-sign rule as the candidate list, once its list performance has been measured in the AQ3 test, the signs as the reasons shown to staff and the AQ1 method as the measure of completion, while they leave the effect of contact as the main question the pilot must answer. Section 12 sets out the limits of these answers and the data still needed.
