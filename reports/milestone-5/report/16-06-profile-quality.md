## Appendix VI – Data Profile and Data Quality Issue Log

This appendix supports Section 6. It gives the keys and distributions of the profile, the Data Quality Issue Log with the evidence of each issue, the findings on duplicates, missing values, validity and outliers, and the temporal coverage, representativeness and sensitive attributes of OULAD.

**Unit and keys.** OULAD holds 22 module runs of seven Open University modules from 2013 and 2014, with 32,593 enrolment records that belong to 28,785 distinct students. The unit of analysis is the enrolment, one student in one module run, because the outcome, the dates and the assessed tasks are all recorded per enrolment; finer tables, such as the daily click records, are summed to the enrolment before they are joined to it. The six tables that carry a key have no duplicate key, and no row of a child table lacks its parent row; only the click table, which records platform activity, has no key at all. The profile ran on all 10.9 million rows of the seven raw tables rather than on a sample, and thirteen of its figures were recounted independently by separate code, with all thirteen in agreement.

**Outcome and time.** The final result is recorded at the end of each run as Pass (12,361 enrolments), Withdrawn (10,156), Fail (7,052) or Distinction (3,024). We define non-completion as Fail or Withdrawn and completion as Pass or Distinction, so the target joins two different ways of not finishing. The joint target is kept because the primary KPI counts both a fail and a withdrawal as non-completion (Section 2.1), and AQ1 reports the two outcomes separately (Section 11.2). Time is counted in days from day 0, the official start of each run, and week n covers days 7(n−1) to 7n−1. The two data cut-offs of decision D1, whom the course lecturers, lecturer-advisors and the support office contact first at each review point, are therefore day 13, the end of week 2, and day 27, the end of week 4.

**Outcome balance.** Of the 28,061 enrolments still registered at day 14, the population of the primary KPI, 54.7% completed. Among the enrolments registered at the cut-offs, non-completion is 45.3% at day 13 and 44.2% at day 27, and it ranges from 24.9% to 58.3% between runs in the KPI population; even the smaller class holds at least 92 enrolments in every run. The classes are therefore close in size, and no resampling or reweighting is needed for any question. The mix of non-completion differs by module: withdrawal is highest in the CCC runs, at 43–46%, while GGG has the fewest withdrawals but the most failures, so a single non-completion rate hides different patterns that AQ1 reports separately.

**Distributions.** Activity is strongly skewed: the median enrolment has 602 clicks, 3,365 enrolments have none, 2,503 lie beyond the upper fence of the interquartile range, and a single record of one student on one resource on one day reaches 6,977 clicks. Scores lean the other way, with a median of 80, and 45.8% of CMA scores against 17.4% of TMA scores fall on a multiple of ten, which reflects how the two kinds of task are marked. Study load reaches 655 credits against a 99th percentile of 240. These shapes decide the encoding of Section 7.1: counts enter models as logarithms, and no value is removed.

The profile of Section 6 shows a large, well-keyed dataset whose outcome classes are close in size; its quality findings decide which of its records can be used as they stand and which would bias a rate, the target or a feature if they were ignored. Milestone 3 turned the profile into a Data Quality Issue Log of nineteen issues, DQ01 to DQ19, each with its evidence, its impact on the questions, a severity and a decision. The severity is judged against the questions: High marks an issue that would change a reported rate, the target or a feature at a cut-off if it were ignored; Medium one that would change a measure by a known amount; and Low one that touches few records or a field that no question uses. **Duplicates and grain.** Of the click rows, 787,170 (7.39%) repeat another row exactly, yet collapsing them would remove only 3.19% of all clicks. Equal click counts occur in 31.5% of pairs of rows for the same student, resource and day, against 24.0% expected by chance from each resource's own counts, so the repeats are more than chance would produce, yet the data cannot show whether they are double logging or repeated visits. As a result, every row is kept, raw clicks are the declared primary measure, and a de-duplicated twin of each click feature allows a sensitivity check (DQ01). The duplicate share is lower in the development year than in the test year, 6.82% of rows in the 2013 runs against 7.87% in 2014, so the twin also shows whether a click feature would shift between years for a reason unrelated to students. In addition, the click table holds several rows for 1,614,505 student-resource-days, so clicks are summed by enrolment and day before any use (DQ02). A direct join of submissions with click rows would return 89,347,197 rows, and an inner join would drop the 3,365 enrolments without any click, whose lack of activity is itself one of the signs under study; tables are therefore summed and left-joined from the enrolment table (DQ17).

**Missing values.** The deprivation band, from the Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD), is blank for 1,111 enrolments (3.41%), and these students pass in 65.7% of cases against 46.6% of the others. Because the blanks relate to the outcome, they are kept as their own "Missing" group rather than filled (DQ12), and the fairness audit of Section 9.2 reports that group on its own. In addition, 45 registration days and 173 TMA scores are missing (DQ13, DQ11), and 18 of the 24 exams have no result row at all, by design of the dataset (DQ08). No missing value is imputed in the prepared views; Section 7 describes the indicators and the filling that happens only inside model fitting.

**Validity.** Six inconsistencies were found, and none was corrected by changing a recorded value: 93 Withdrawn enrolments without an unregistration day, 87 of them in October 2014 runs (DQ04); 58 registrations after day 14 (DQ05); 1,909 banked results dated −1 (DQ07); 98,745 clicks and 588 submissions after the student unregistered (DQ03); 32,991 of 69,956 CMA submissions and 16,320 of 97,088 TMA submissions recorded as late (DQ10); and one deprivation band label written in two forms. Activity after unregistration is cut at the unregistration day, so a withdrawn student is never credited with engagement after leaving, and banked results, carried over from an earlier attempt, are counted as known at registration but never timed.

**Outliers.** Activity is strongly right-skewed, with 2,503 enrolments beyond the upper fence and a single record of one student on one resource on one day reaching 6,977 clicks, and study load reaches 655 credits. These are plausible records of very active or heavily loaded students, so no value is removed or capped, and models sensitive to scale use logarithms (DQ16).

**Issues that could change a conclusion.** Three issues are rated High, and each would change a result if ignored. The missing exam results (DQ08) would count the exam as missed for every student of the five modules without exam results if exams were counted as tasks, so only TMAs and CMAs count as assessed tasks, and AQ1's first missed task means the first missed TMA or CMA. The few tasks due by the cut-offs (DQ18) would leave a task-only early-sign rule silent in most runs: a TMA or CMA falls due by day 13 in only 1 of the 22 runs and by day 27 in 16. In the 9 development runs of 2013 the first task falls due on day 19 at the earliest, so no 2013 run has a task due by day 13, and 7 of the 9 have one due by day 27; EEE 2013J and GGG 2013J have their first task on days 33 and 61. The early-sign rule (C10) was therefore restated with an activity part, and AQ2's task-based signs are estimated only in the 7 runs where a task falls due. The narrow population (DQ19) limits every claim, so the scope is stated with each result and comparisons are made within module (Section 6.2).

Two Medium issues could also move a conclusion by a known amount, and the analysis keeps both visible. Counting late CMAs in the early-sign rule would raise the share of students it flags at day 27 from 28.8% to 41.2%, and the meaning of a late CMA in the 2013–2014 runs is not documented; the rule therefore uses TMA lateness only (DQ10), and counting late CMAs becomes a sensitivity run of the late-task sign of AQ2 (Appendix XIII). The click duplicates (DQ01) carry 3.19% of all clicks, so they could inflate activity measures by about that share, which the de-duplicated twin tests.

These findings produced the changes C09 to C13 of the Controlled Change Log, the list of every change to the proposal with its reason in the data: the population rule (C09), the restated early-sign rule (C10), the ranking form of AQ4 (C11), the several-modules sign limited to 2014 (C12) and the list metrics measured against the attainable maximum (C13). None of them alters the Business Goal or the decisions D1 to D3; they change how a question is measured, which Section 3.2 traces question by question. Section 6.2 turns to the remaining dimensions of data understanding: when the data were recorded, whom they represent and which fields need protection.

**Temporal coverage.** The data cover the February (B) and October (J) runs of 2013 and 2014, with platform activity from about four weeks before the start of a run to week 39. Weekly clicks per enrolled student rise before the start and peak in week 3, so the two cut-offs at day 13 and day 27 fall in the weeks of highest activity. Two days of all February 2014 runs, days 218 and 219, carry no click at all; this logging gap is treated as missing rather than as zero activity (DQ15), and it lies far after both cut-offs. Assessed tasks fall due late in comparison: a TMA or CMA is due by day 13 in only 1 of the 22 runs and by day 27 in 16 (DQ18), which is why the early-sign rule has an activity part and why the week-3 list of decision D1 rests on activity alone.

The structure of the runs over time fixes the split of every predictive analysis. Nine pairs of module and start month ran in both years, while module CCC and the February runs of EEE and GGG ran only in 2014. Each module therefore has at most one earlier run of the same start month, and the only way to test a model on a later run of the same course, as the university would use it on a later semester, is to train on 2013 and test on 2014. Of the 13 test runs, the 9 whose module and start month also ran in 2013 test that claim directly, and the other 4 test transfer to a module or start month the model has not seen (Section 9.1).

**Representativeness.** OULAD is a subset of Open University students of 2013–2014, reduced by anonymisation, with modules chosen partly because many students failed them. The students learn at a distance: 70.4% are under 35, and 63.1% study 60 credits or fewer. The module mix is uneven, with women forming 11.5% of the enrolments of EEE and 88.4% of those of BBB, and completion in the day-14 population of the 22 runs ranges from 45.7% in CCC to 73.8% in AAA. Three consequences follow for every result. First, results describe OULAD runs and not the university's courses; OULAD is a stand-in, and each result must be checked on the university's own records before it informs decisions D1 to D3 (DQ19). Second, groups are compared within module, because a comparison across modules would mix the effect of a group with the effect of the module's subject and student mix. Third, the development year is narrower than the whole dataset: the 9 runs of 2013 cover 6 of the 7 modules, so module CCC appears only in the test year.

**Sensitive fields.** Sensitive attributes are those that describe protected or vulnerable groups, such as gender, age, disability, socioeconomic status and location. OULAD holds six of them, and Table A.16 gives each with its role in the analysis and the caution its use requires.

**Table A.16. Sensitive attributes and their use**

| Attribute | Role | Known group sizes | Caution in use |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Gender | Audit only | Women 11.5% of EEE, 88.4% of BBB | Compared within module |
| Age band | Audit only | Aged 55 or over: 216 enrolments | Too small for run-level rates; audited across runs |
| Declared disability | Audit only (AQ4 fairness group) | 3,164 enrolments | Sensitive personal data in the pilot |
| Deprivation band (IMD) | Audit only (AQ4 fairness group) | 1,111 enrolments with a missing band | Describes the home area, not the student; "Missing" reported as its own group |
| Region | Audit only | – | Not a predictor |
| Highest education | Predictor, also audited | Postgraduates: 313 enrolments | Groups audited like the others |

We set 20 students as the lower limit for reporting a group rate run by run, and smaller groups, such as students aged 55 or over (216 enrolments) and postgraduates (313), are audited across runs. The deprivation band needs a further caution: it describes the area where the student lives rather than the student, and Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland use deprivation indices that are not directly comparable with England's, so a band difference is a difference between areas.

All six attributes stay in every prepared view: five as audit attributes outside every predictor set, and highest education as a predictor whose groups are also audited. They are kept rather than removed because bias in a prediction can arise through proxies, other fields that carry the same information, even when no demographic attribute is an input, and bias cannot be audited without data on group membership [6]. Removing the attributes would therefore hide bias rather than prevent it. The need for the audit is concrete on this dataset: a published fairness study of at-risk prediction on OULAD reproduced a disparity of five to six percentage points in favour of male students [7]. Section 9.2 sets the audit that the proposed ranking must pass.

**Privacy.** Privacy rules apply at two levels. OULAD was anonymised with k-anonymity, k = 5, meaning that every combination of the attributes that could identify a student is shared by at least five students. Under Vietnam's Personal Data Protection Law No. 91/2025/QH15, de-identified data are no longer personal data and re-identifying them is prohibited [8], so OULAD is never linked to any other source. For the pilot on the university's own records the position differs: those records identify students, and the same law protects health information, such as a declared disability, by requiring the student's consent for its collection and processing [8]; the pilot therefore needs its own legal basis before any student record is prepared. With the data understood and their limits stated, Section 7 describes how the raw tables become the prepared data on which every question is answered.
