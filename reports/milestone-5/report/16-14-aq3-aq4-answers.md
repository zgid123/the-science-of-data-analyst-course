## Appendix XIV – AQ3 and AQ4 Results, Final Answers and Decision Support in Detail

This appendix supports Sections 10, 11.4 and 11.5. It gives the results of AQ3 and AQ4 against the criteria Milestone 4 fixed for them, the final answers by question with every cell written out, and the decision support for D1–D3 with the hypothesis each option leaves to test.

Sections 11.2 and 11.3 answered the descriptive and diagnostic questions on real data from 2013 and 2014. The predictive question (AQ3) and the prescriptive ranking question (AQ4) are different in kind: their answers come from a model developed and frozen on the 9 runs of 2013 and tested once on the 13 runs of 2014, the Level 2 prototype of Section 10. Every number below comes from that single test; the development folds served only the choice between candidates.

### AQ3: estimating non-completion at the end of weeks 2 and 4

AQ3 asks how well non-completion can be estimated from the data up to the end of week 2 and of week 4, compared with the early-sign rule, when a model built on the 2013 runs is applied to the 2014 runs (Section 3). The primary model is a penalised logistic regression on the 19 usable primary predictors, one model per cut-off, compared with three baselines: the early-sign rule (C10) ranked by its number of signs (B2), a logistic model on background variables only (B3) and a random list. In development on the 9 runs of 2013, gradient-boosted trees and the extended predictor set changed pooled normalised recall by −0.001 to −0.002, far from the margin of 0.03 fixed in advance, so the primary model stayed. Its penalty was tuned by the log loss of an inner leave-one-run-out loop, because the plan named no tuning measure (C18).

Gate S1 is met on all 13 runs of 2014 (15,724 enrolments registered at day 27, 6,911 non-completers). At day 27 and 5 contacts per 20 students, the model's pooled normalised recall is 0.725 against 0.704 for the rule-ranked list: a difference of +0.021 with a paired two-level bootstrap interval of +0.006 to +0.036, a one-sided sign-flip p-value of 0.0035, and 11 wins and 2 losses over the 13 runs. Normalised recall divides the recall of a list by the most a list of that length could reach; here every list of 5 per 20 could be filled with non-completers, so normalised recall equals precision (S2): 72.5% of the model's listed students and 70.4% of the rule's did not complete, against 43.9% for a random list. The gain is 82 more non-completers on 3,955 list places, about one per ten tutor groups, so the rule keeps 97% of the model's hits. Discrimination within run tells the same story (ROC-AUC 0.730 against 0.711 for the rule's order; background-only model 0.603). Calibration meets S3 on the pooled estimates (calibration-in-the-large 0.117, slope 0.886) but in only 5 of the 13 runs, so the output stays a rank with reasons and no probability is shown; no recalibration was made on the 2014 runs.

Every sensitivity view keeps the direction of the gain: day 13 +0.021 (+0.002 to +0.034), 3 per 20 +0.031 (+0.009 to +0.052), the 9 runs paired with a 2013 run +0.024 (+0.010 to +0.043), without the students already seen in 2013 +0.020 (+0.006 to +0.037), and without the Withdrawn enrolments lacking a withdrawal day +0.020 (+0.007 to +0.035). In the 4 unpaired runs (+0.013, −0.019 to +0.035) and in module CCC alone (+0.018, −0.006 to +0.046) the interval includes 0, as expected from so few runs (Change Log C16). Lists of 5 per 20 hold 38.6% of later non-completers at day 13 and 41.5% at day 27. The model and the rule share most names; each list holds 903 students the other does not. The model's own picks mostly show no rule sign (21.3% have one), more often had a previous attempt at the module (30.7% against 6.4%), and 59.7% of them did not complete, against 50.6% of the rule's own picks. The main error is a blind spot: 4,193 of the 6,911 non-completers (60.7%) still had normal activity at day 27, at least half their run's median clicks, and the lists hold 19.1% of them (rule 15.6%), against 98.4% of those with no click at all.

### AQ4: a ranked list under assumed capacity

AQ4 asks which enrolments should rank first on a list of an assumed capacity, with what gain over a random list and what recall, overall and by deprivation band and disability (Section 3). The lists are built from the frozen AQ3 scores, 3 or 5 per 20 students per run, ties broken by a fixed random number per student. At 5 per 20 they reach 41.5% of all later non-completers against 25.1% for a random list (lift 1.65) and 40.3% for the rule's list; at 3 per 20, 80.9% of the listed students did not complete. Counted per tutor group of 20, the first contact finds a non-completer 92.4% of the time, the fifth 57.6% and the sixth 55.2%; from the seventh on the yield is 47.3% or less, close to the base rate of 44.0%. Of the 2,868 listed non-completers, 111 withdraw in days 28–34, the week the list arrives, and 1,646 (57.4%) stay to the end and fail, for whom academic support is the relevant help. Each name carries the predictor group that pushes its risk most above average: for 80.7% of listed students a behavioural reason (low activity 46.5%, a missed task 27.2%, a late submission 7.0%), for 14.2% prior education, a background fact a tutor cannot act on and that is sensitive to show.

Rule FR1 flags none of the three confirmatory groups. Students with a declared disability (1,488 at day 27) are reached slightly better than the others (+5.5 points), the most deprived band, 0–10% (1,486), about equally (+1.3), and the missing deprivation band (610) less well (−7.4 points, interval −16.8 to +2.3; Holm-adjusted p = 0.19), so no gap is more than 10 points below the others with an interval below 0. Students aged 55 or over (118) are too few for the confirmatory check and are reported as insufficient data, not cleared. Described rather than tested, women's non-completers are reached less often than men's (38.7% against 43.4%), while their base rate is also lower (41.3% against 45.9%). The absence of a flag means no evidence of a gap at the precision each group's size allows, not a finding that the list is fair. The labels-available variant, the area between ROC curves and the stability of list membership, descriptive extras of the plan, were not computed; they cannot change S1 or FR1.

These results let the course lecturers, the lecturer-advisors and the support office know whom to list first at the week-3 and week-5 review points (D1), but they do not say who should make the contact, with which offer, or whether contact raises completion. A predictive score ranks students by risk; it does not explain why a student struggles, and it does not show that the students at highest risk are those who would benefit most from contact. Those questions need contact records and a comparison design that OULAD cannot provide, as Section 11.5 and Section 12 set out. Read across its rows, the table shows that the project can describe the size and timing of non-completion, name the early signs that go with it and rank students so that a short list holds mostly later non-completers, while it cannot say whether contacting a listed student changes the outcome.

### Why recommendations remain options to test

All four answers rest on observational data, and none of them can show what contact would achieve. Questions of effectiveness are ideally answered by a randomised experiment, and observational data can only try to emulate one [14]; such an emulation fails when the data lack sufficient information on the baseline confounders that drive both the decision and the outcome [14]. OULAD records no contact at all, so it cannot even supply the comparison between contacted and non-contacted students. Even the Open University's own evaluation of its predictive learning analytics, which did involve teacher action, warns that any causal interpretation of its findings should be treated with caution [3]. For these reasons we present every prescriptive statement below as a decision option: a course of action the evidence makes reasonable, together with the hypothesis the pilot would test before the option becomes practice.

For D1, the evidence supports a clear candidate. The AQ3 model passed the single gate fixed in Milestone 4, so its ranking orders the list; the early-sign rule (C10) is defined and interpretable, can be computed from records the university already holds, and keeps 97% of the model's hits, so it is the fallback where a model cannot be maintained. A list longer than about 6 per 20 adds names at almost the base rate, and the 60.7% of non-completers who still look active at week 4 need a later review point rather than a longer list. What the list cannot yet support is the second half of D1, the choice of person and offer, because no data in OULAD record contacts; the option is to leave that choice open and randomise in the pilot, so that the pilot answers it.

For D2, the AQ1 rates show where non-completion concentrates in OULAD, and the AQ2 signs show where students stop taking part early. The option of placing early support time where both are highest is reasonable, but it depends on the same pattern appearing in the university's courses, since OULAD describes UK distance-learning modules of 2013–2014 rather than the program's online and blended courses, although the module pattern holds across the two years of OULAD. For D3, the project offers a measurement method rather than a verdict: a completion rate computed by one rule for pilot and comparison courses, a ranking gate fixed in advance and a fairness audit. An extension close to a one-way door should wait for a pilot result, even a negative one, because the pilot shows at small scale which part of the path works.

Taken together, the answers support preparing the pilot with the AQ3 ranking as the list and the early-sign rule as its fallback, the behavioural signs as the reasons shown to staff and the AQ1 method as the measure of completion, while they leave the effect of contact as the main question the pilot must answer. Section 12 sets out the limits of these answers and the data still needed.
