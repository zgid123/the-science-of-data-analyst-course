## 8.1. Baselines, Candidate Methods and Primary Proposal

The Modeling Briefs of Section 8 fix the technical task of each question; each task now receives the baseline that any method must beat, its candidate methods and a primary proposal with its reason, chosen by criteria rather than by the popularity of an algorithm.

**Criteria.** Each candidate is judged on the eight criteria that the course makes compulsory: fit to the question, whether the method directly produces the output the question needs; fit to the data, whether sample size, feature types, sparsity, imbalance, time structure and granularity suit it; assumptions, what the method requires of the data and whether the data roughly meet it; interpretability, how much of the logic the users need to understand; complexity against the baseline, what a more complex method is expected to add; robustness and reproducibility, whether it can be evaluated stably, rerun and kept free of leakage; ethics and fairness, whether its errors could harm a group of students and whether it uses a sensitive attribute; and actionability, whether its output supports the decision it serves. Four findings from the literature on prediction systems, summarised in Milestone 4 (Section 3.1), settle most ties: a simple heuristic often delivers much of the gain, so predictive work starts from a rule baseline and a simple initial model; when many models reach about the same error, the simpler one can be chosen without loss; complexity is costly to maintain over time; and for high-stakes decisions about people, an inherently interpretable model is preferable to a black box explained afterwards.

**AQ1: grouped rates.** AQ1 is descriptive, so it is answered with descriptive statistics rather than machine learning. The baseline is the pooled rate, set beside a second denominator, the rate over all enrolments rather than only those registered at day 14, so that the comparison shows which rates depend on the population definition, and the rate without the enrolments flagged for an unknown withdrawal or registration day. The primary method is a table of grouped rates: non-completion and the four outcome shares by module, run and start month, each with its events, its denominator and a 95% Wilson score interval, and the cumulative share of enrolments with a first missed TMA or CMA by week, compared by outcome. The difference between years is computed as the 2014 rate minus the 2013 rate with a 95% Newcombe hybrid score interval, on the all-enrolment denominator that the published 2014 counts allow (Change Log C15). On the 9 runs of 2013 the two denominators are 12,076 enrolments registered at day 14 and all 13,529 enrolments.

**AQ2: association within module run.** The baseline is the unadjusted rate difference per sign, the gap in non-completion between students with and without the sign. The primary approach is a logistic regression of non-completion on the signs with a fixed effect per module run, adjustment for previous attempts and credits studied, and sign × module terms, so that each sign's association is estimated within the run and may differ by module. Because every run of the modules is observed rather than sampled, the model uses robust standard errors without clustering; the intervals therefore describe these 9 runs, and the variation that matters when the signs are applied to later runs shows in the module estimates (Section 11.3.3) rather than in the intervals. The mixed-effects alternative is set aside, because 6 modules and 9 training runs are too few to estimate the variance between them. The effect is reported as the average risk difference in percentage points, with the odds ratio beside it, because at a non-completion rate near 44% odds ratios read larger than the corresponding risk ratios. The signs are correlated, so their mutually adjusted estimates are partial effects, smaller than each sign's own gap; both are therefore shown side by side. The smallest training run holds 369 enrolments with 91 non-completers.

In Milestone 5 we ran this specification on the 11,811 enrolments registered at day 27 in the 9 runs of 2013, and five choices that the plan left open were fixed before the model was fitted. The signs are five: a task due by day 27 missed, a TMA submitted late, no click on days 21–27, clicks on days 0–27 below the median of the same run, and no click before day 0; the last one is named among the inputs of the AQ2 Modeling Brief of Milestone 4, beside the four kinds of sign in the question's wording, which stays as Milestone 4 decided it. The relative-activity sign uses the literal reading "below the run median", with "below half the median" as a sensitivity run. Previous attempts and credits (per 30 credits) enter linearly. The robust covariance is the heteroskedasticity-consistent form HC1. Sign × module terms are coded as deviations from the average module, so that the reported odds ratio is the one at the average module, with the module values beside it. The task-based signs are defined in the 7 runs with a task due by day 27 and in 4 modules, the click-based signs in all 9 runs and 6 modules, and the model has 37 parameters. Each sign appears in three columns: unadjusted, adjusted for run, background and its own module terms, and mutually adjusted for all signs. Section 11.3 reports the results.

**AQ3: proposed predictive model.** Three baselines are proposed, because a model is useful only if it beats what staff could do without it: the early-sign rule as frozen in C10, ranked by its number of signs with ties broken by lower relative activity; a background-only logistic model that uses module, start month, previous attempts, credits, education and registration lead but no activity; and a random list. The primary proposal is a penalised logistic regression on the 19 primary predictors, one model per cut-off, with 23 parameters once module and start month are coded. Counts enter as the logarithm of one plus the count, the registration lead is median-filled with a missing indicator, and every predictor is standardised, with each of these transforms refitted inside each development fold. The challenger is gradient-boosted trees, which read the predictors untransformed and may capture interactions the linear model misses; they, or the extended predictor set, replace the primary approach only if they beat it by the margin fixed in advance (Section 9.1). A discrete-time survival model stays an extension, because decision D1 acts at two fixed cut-offs and needs no timing, and the target joins Fail, decided only at the end of the run, with Withdrawn.

The rationale for the primary proposal follows the criteria. Staff must see why a student is listed, and a logistic model gives each student's reasons from the same coefficients that rank the student. The data would support it amply: about 230 non-completers per parameter, and 11,811 training enrolments at day 27, about seven times the 1,731 that a published minimum-sample-size criterion for prediction models requires for this setting (Milestone 4, Section 3.3). The classes are close in size, so no resampling is needed, and calibration would be checked rather than assumed.

**AQ4: proposed ranking.** The baselines are a random list, whose expected recall equals k/n for a list of length k in a run of n enrolments, and the rule-ranked list of the same length. The primary proposal is a top-k list per run from the AQ3 scores, where k follows the assumed capacity of 3 or 5 contacts per group of 20 students (a scenario value, Section 2.1), that is 15% or 25% of the enrolments registered at the cut-off, with one list per run. The alternative, risk tiers set from capacity, would group students without an order inside each tier and is kept as a candidate only. The list would give staff ranks and each student's main contributing signs, and probabilities only when calibration holds; the member of staff decides whom to contact. Ranking students by the expected benefit of a contact is rejected, because it needs randomised or otherwise unconfounded data on contacts and their outcomes, which OULAD does not hold. The assumption that the order of risk matches the order of need therefore stays untested until a randomised pilot.

**Traceability of the choices.** Each choice traces back to its question: AQ1 to grouped rates with intervals, AQ2 to a logistic regression with run fixed effects, AQ3 to a penalised logistic regression judged by normalised recall at capacity, and AQ4 to a top-k list per run with reasons. The method changes only how a question is measured where a Change Log entry records it, namely C14 for AQ2 and C15 for AQ1, both of which protect the independence of the AQ3 test. Section 9 sets how each of these methods would be judged good enough.
