## 6.2. Temporal Coverage, Representativeness and Sensitive Fields

The quality findings of Section 6.1 settle which records can be used as they stand; three further properties of the data decide how far any result can travel: when the data were recorded, whom they represent, and which fields describe protected groups.

**Temporal coverage.** The data cover the February (B) and October (J) runs of 2013 and 2014, with platform activity from about four weeks before the start of a run to week 39. Weekly clicks per enrolled student rise before the start and peak in week 3, so the two cut-offs at day 13 and day 27 fall in the weeks of highest activity. Two days of all February 2014 runs, days 218 and 219, carry no click at all; this logging gap is treated as missing rather than as zero activity (DQ15), and it lies far after both cut-offs. Assessed tasks fall due late in comparison: a TMA or CMA is due by day 13 in only 1 of the 22 runs and by day 27 in 16 (DQ18), which is why the early-sign rule has an activity part and why the week-3 list of decision D1 rests on activity alone.

The structure of the runs over time fixes the split of every predictive analysis. Nine pairs of module and start month ran in both years, while module CCC and the February runs of EEE and GGG ran only in 2014. Each module therefore has at most one earlier run of the same start month, and the only way to test a model on a later run of the same course, as the university would use it on a later semester, is to train on 2013 and test on 2014. Of the 13 test runs, the 9 whose module and start month also ran in 2013 test that claim directly, and the other 4 test transfer to a module or start month the model has not seen (Section 9.1).

**Representativeness.** OULAD is a subset of OU students of 2013–2014, reduced by anonymisation, with modules chosen partly because many students failed them. The students learn at a distance: 70.4% are under 35, and 63.1% study 60 credits or fewer. The module mix is uneven, with women forming 11.5% of the enrolments of EEE and 88.4% of those of BBB, and completion in the day-14 population of the 22 runs ranges from 45.7% in CCC to 73.8% in AAA. Three consequences follow for every result. First, results describe OULAD runs and not the university's courses; OULAD is a stand-in, and each result must be checked on the university's own records before it informs decisions D1 to D3 (DQ19). Second, groups are compared within module, because a comparison across modules would mix the effect of a group with the effect of the module's subject and student mix. Third, the development year is narrower than the whole dataset: the 9 runs of 2013 cover 6 of the 7 modules, so module CCC appears only in the test year.

**Sensitive fields.** Sensitive attributes are those that describe protected or vulnerable groups, such as gender, age, disability, socioeconomic status and location. OULAD holds six of them, and Table 6.3 gives each with its role in the analysis and the caution its use requires.

**Table 6.3. Sensitive attributes and their use**

| Attribute | Role | Known group sizes | Caution in use |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Gender | Audit only | Women 11.5% of EEE, 88.4% of BBB | Compared within module |
| Age band | Audit only | Aged 55 or over: 216 enrolments | Too small for run-level rates; audited across runs |
| Declared disability | Audit only (AQ4 fairness group) | 3,164 enrolments | Sensitive personal data in the pilot |
| Deprivation band (IMD) | Audit only (AQ4 fairness group) | 1,111 enrolments with a missing band | Describes the home area, not the student; "Missing" reported as its own group |
| Region | Audit only | – | Not a predictor |
| Highest education | Predictor, also audited | Postgraduates: 313 enrolments | Groups audited like the others |

We set 20 students as the lower limit for reporting a group rate run by run, and smaller groups, such as students aged 55 or over (216 enrolments) and postgraduates (313), are audited across runs. The deprivation band needs a further caution: it describes the area where the student lives rather than the student, and Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland use deprivation indices that are not directly comparable with England's, so a band difference is a difference between areas.

All six attributes stay in every prepared view: five as audit attributes outside every predictor set, and highest education as a predictor whose groups are also audited. They are kept rather than removed because bias in a prediction can arise through proxies, other fields that carry the same information, even when no demographic attribute is an input, and bias cannot be audited without data on group membership [S906]. Removing the attributes would therefore hide bias rather than prevent it. The need for the audit is concrete on this dataset: a published fairness study of at-risk prediction on OULAD reproduced a disparity of five to six percentage points in favour of male students [S907]. Section 9.2 sets the audit that the proposed ranking must pass.

**Privacy.** Privacy rules apply at two levels. OULAD was anonymised with k-anonymity, k = 5, meaning that every combination of the attributes that could identify a student is shared by at least five students. Under Vietnam's Personal Data Protection Law No. 91/2025/QH15, de-identified data are no longer personal data and re-identifying them is prohibited [S913], so OULAD is never linked to any other source. For the pilot on the university's own records the position differs: those records identify students, and the same law protects health information, such as a declared disability, by requiring the student's consent for its collection and processing [S913]; the pilot therefore needs its own legal basis before any student record is prepared. With the data understood and their limits stated, Section 7 describes how the raw tables become the prepared data on which every question is answered.
