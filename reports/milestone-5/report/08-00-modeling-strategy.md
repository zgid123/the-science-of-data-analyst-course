# 8. Modeling and Analysis Strategy

The prepared dataset of Section 7 fixes what each question can use; the modeling strategy fixes how each question is answered, by mapping it to an analytics type, a technical task, a baseline, candidate methods and a primary proposal with its rationale. Milestone 4 designed this strategy at Level 1, the compulsory level of the course: methods and their evaluation were specified, and no model was trained (Milestone 4, Sections 2–3). In Milestone 5 we ran the descriptive and diagnostic analyses of AQ1 and AQ2 on the 9 runs of 2013 exactly as specified, and Section 11 reports their results; the predictive and ranking questions AQ3 and AQ4 remain proposals, written in the proposal tense, with no model metric.

**Readiness at entry.** The questions entered the modeling stage in the state Milestone 3 rated them. AQ1 was ready on the enrolment and assessed-task views; AQ2 was ready for association only on the day-27 feature view; AQ3 was ready, with the day-13 list resting on activity alone because almost no task falls due by day 13; and AQ4 was ready as a ranking, with the choice of contact deferred to a pilot. The six entry conditions of the course for modeling held for AQ1 to AQ3 and for the ranking part of AQ4: prepared views with clear traceability, a stable target and features, defined leakage controls and split, quality issues handled or kept on purpose, no synthetic data as evidence, and data that rebuild from the raw files. Milestone 4 then checked its own entry gate of five conditions, approved questions, prepared dataset, leakage controls, known limitations and traceability, and all five held, so the questions could be modelled as they stood, with the contact part of AQ4 left deferred.

**Why a ranking is needed.** The early-sign rule (C10) already names students at risk without any model: it flags 20.2% of enrolled students at day 13 and 28.8% at day 27 over the 22 runs. Staff capacity, assumed at 3 or 5 contacts per group of 20 students (a scenario value, Section 2.1), covers 15% or 25% of a run, so in many runs the rule names more students than a list can hold, and staff need an order of whom to contact first. The rule alone cannot set that order, which is the task of AQ3 and AQ4. A prescriptive answer in the full sense, which contact to make and with what effect, is out of reach, because the data hold no action, no cost and no outcome after a contact; the only prescriptive output OULAD supports is a list of a given size under an assumed capacity.

**Development data.** All model development uses the 9 runs of 2013. At day 27 they hold 11,811 enrolments, of whom 5,245 (44.4%) did not complete, against 15,724 enrolments in the 13 test runs of 2014; at day 13 the figures are 12,080 enrolments with 5,519 non-completers, against 15,989 test enrolments. The outcomes of the test runs stay unopened until a single final test. Two entries of the Controlled Change Log keep them unopened in the analyses that do not need them: AQ2 is estimated on the 2013 runs and replicated on the 2014 runs only after the AQ3 test (C14), so that no test outcome is seen while the predictive model is chosen; and AQ1 is computed on the 9 runs of 2013, with the year comparison drawn from the 2014 outcome counts per run that Milestone 3 already published (C15). The 22-run figures quoted elsewhere, such as the 28,061 enrolments of the KPI population and their 54.7% completion, come from Milestone 3 and are not recomputed.

**Purpose first.** Before any algorithm is named, each question's purpose fixes how its result may be read. Explanatory modeling tests causal explanations, whereas predictive modeling aims at predicting new or future observations, and rankings count as predictions [S900]. A regression used only to capture associations, with neither a causal nor a predictive aim, is descriptive in the same framework [S900]. AQ2 therefore keeps the diagnostic level with association as its technical task and is never read as cause, while AQ3 and AQ4 are predictions and must be judged on data the model has not seen, because explanatory power does not imply predictive power [S900].

**Modeling Briefs.** For each question, a Modeling Brief records eight attributes before any algorithm is named: the question and its wording, its analytics level, the decision it supports, the unit of analysis, the outcome or target, the information allowed as input with its time window, the expected output and the main constraint. Naming the expected output matters, because the same binary outcome can be reported as classes, as probabilities or as a ranking, and each form needs its own measure of quality. Table 8.1 condenses the four briefs to the attributes that decide the method.

**Technical tasks.** The briefs translate into four technical tasks. AQ1 is aggregation: rates and shares grouped by module, run, start month and year, each with its denominator and an interval. AQ2 is association within module run: how much more often students with a sign by day 27 fail to complete than comparable students of the same run without it. AQ3 is binary classification at two prediction points, the ends of day 13 and day 27, trained on earlier runs and tested on a later one, as the model would be used on a run still in progress. AQ4 is ranking under a fixed capacity, where the quality of the top of the list, the part on which staff act, matters more than the predicted values themselves. AQ3 and AQ4 share one target and one population, so AQ4 reuses the AQ3 scores and adds only the rule that cuts the ranking at the length capacity allows. Section 8.1 sets the baselines and candidate methods for each task and the primary proposal with its rationale.
