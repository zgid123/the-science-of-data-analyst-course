## Appendix XVI – AI Decision Log and Reflection

This appendix supports Section 13. It gives the AI Decision Log of Milestones 1–5 in the columns of the course brief and the full reflection on how the output of each AI role was verified.

Section 12 stated the limits of the evidence; the way that evidence was produced also includes the use of generative AI in every milestone. We used generative AI as a simulated stakeholder, a research and code assistant, a critic and a writing partner, and in every role under one rule: AI was never a source of data, statistics, licence information or results, and every fact it offered was checked against a saved source, the data files or code that we ran. For each milestone we record what AI was used for, what it suggested, how we verified or changed its output and what we decided, and we reflect on whether AI supported our reasoning or risked replacing evidence.

Table A.43 gives the AI Decision Log. The rows "M1 / M5" to "M4 / M5" summarise the AI use of each earlier milestone and how its result is carried into this final report; the rows for Milestone 5 record the AI use in the integration itself. The last row covers the drafting and review of the full report.

**Milestones 1 to 4 in brief.** In Milestone 1, AI personas answered as stakeholders to an AI interviewer and a reviewer gave twelve critiques; each quote was checked against its transcript, nine critiques were accepted, two in part and one rejected, and the interviews count as simulated needs, not verified ones. In Milestone 2, a requirement critic and a selection reviewer gave 22 critiques, every dataset fact came from the official source, and the gates came to require a dated withdrawal and dated tasks. In Milestone 3, AI drafted the preparation scripts, kept only after review and an independent recount, and a separate leakage auditor gave eight findings, all acted on. In Milestone 4, three isolated reviewers gave 24 critiques, which added a switching rule with a margin fixed in advance and fixed gate S1 on all 13 test runs (C16).

Across the five milestones, the roles of AI changed while the rule stayed the same. In Milestone 1 AI played stakeholders and challenged the proposal, in Milestone 2 it helped search for and read dataset documentation, in Milestone 3 it took the seven roles of the brief around the data and the code, with a leakage auditor besides, in Milestone 4 it served as three isolated reviewers of the method, and in Milestone 5 it extracted evidence, wrote analysis code and drafted the integration documents and the report sections. In each case its output was a proposal for us to verify, never a finding. This follows the UNESCO guidance on generative AI in education and research, which holds that generative AI cannot be trusted to be accurate [20], that its errors go unnoticed unless the user knows the topic well [20], and that it makes up things that do not exist [20]. The guidance concludes that learners and researchers should never accept its information at face value [20] and that it can never be an authoritative source of knowledge [20].

Verification took a different form in each role, matched to what could go wrong. Simulated interviews could put words into a persona's mouth, so each quote was checked against its transcript, and the interviews are reported as simulated: they show plausible needs, not verified ones. A literature summary could misstate a source, so each statement was checked against a saved copy with a verbatim quotation. Code could compute the wrong thing silently, so every script was rerun, tested on fixtures and compared with an independent recount; in Milestone 5 the AQ1 and AQ2 results were accepted only after the automated consistency checks passed, a byte-identical rerun and an independent recomputation agreed. A critic could argue from general advice rather than from our data, so critics had to cite the file and key behind every number and were confined to the brief, named appendices or read-only queries. Sophisticated outputs need skilled human input and critical evaluation before use [20], and these checks were how we supplied it.

The reviews helped most where the AI critic had to argue from the data rather than from general advice. The Milestone 3 auditor found real errors in our preparation code, among them tasks due before registration counted as missed and a leakage check that could not fail, and the Milestone 4 reviewer found that the 2013 runs hold 6 modules, not 7, which changed the AQ2 criterion to a share of modules. Requiring a quoted source for every score and a file check for every undocumented fact turned AI assistance into evidence we can stand behind. Rejections were equally part of the work. We rejected the critique that the Business Goal should not mention early identification, did not adopt the imbalance argument for precision–recall curves because non-completion is 44–46%, and did not look at the outcomes of repeat test students although a critic asked for them, because that would have broken the independent test.

Human ownership shows in the decisions that only we could take. We chose to keep the 2014 runs closed and to answer AQ1 and AQ2 on 2013 only, to keep one early-sign rule for OULAD and the pilot, to report AQ3 and AQ4 as designs rather than run a prototype in this milestone (Section 10), and to state every association as an association. Every result the report states was produced by code that we reran and checked, and no number stated by an AI is used without that check. Reproducibility supported this ownership: the FAIR principles ask that data be findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable [27], and they apply also to the algorithms, tools and workflows that produce the data [27], so every analysis step is a script that reruns to the same output, and the Final Project Package carries the code, the data instructions and this log. The project also follows the phases of the Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining (CRISP-DM), which remains the de-facto process model for data-mining projects [28], while adding what most such studies leave out, a planned deployment phase [28], here the randomised pilot of Section 12.1. AI therefore supported our reasoning by challenging it and by speeding up routine work, while the evidence came from the data, the sources and the code we ran. Section 14 draws the project together.
