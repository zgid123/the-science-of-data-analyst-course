# S111 – Muljana & Luo (2019), Factors contributing to student retention in online learning: a systematic literature review

## Summary in my own words

Muljana and Luo review 40 empirical studies (2010–2018) on why online students in higher education do not complete and what institutions can do. They open from the gap between the growth of online learning and its lower completion and retention rates compared with face-to-face courses. The factors they find are institutional support, programme difficulty, sense of belonging, learning facilitation, course design, student behavioural characteristics and demographic or personal variables. Their leading recommendation is early intervention ("catch them early") using pre-college and early-semester data, early alert systems in the LMS, and collaboration among several stakeholders. They also warn that institutions define and measure retention inconsistently, so the scope of a retention measure must be fixed before causes are studied.

## Key facts

- Scope of the review – "A systematic literature review was conducted on 40 studies published between 2010 and 2018."
- Online retention is lower than face-to-face – "student retention rates are significantly lower than those in the traditional environment"
- Xu D. & Jaggars S. S. (2011a, 2011b; Community College Research Center reports on Washington State and Virginia community colleges) report online course completion 8–14 points lower – "Completion rates in online courses are historically lower, about 8-14%, than in the traditional face-to-face courses"
- Moore J. C. & Fetzner M. J. (2009; Journal of Asynchronous Learning Networks) state a 10–20% gap – "Moore and Fetzner (2009) contended that the completion rates in online classes are claimed to be 10-20% lower than in traditional ones"
- Friðriksdóttir K. (2018; Computer Assisted Language Learning, 31(1–2)) on 43,000 Icelandic Online students: blended mode completes better – "the completion rates of blended learning mode were significantly higher than those of other online modes"
- Factors found – "Revealed factors include institutional support, the level difficulty of the programs, promotion of a sense of belonging, facilitation of learning, course design, student behavioral characteristics, and demographic variables along with other personal variables."
- Moore C. & Greenland S. (2017; Journal of Open, Flexible and Distance Learning) – work commitments of online students meet non-supportive extension policies – "Online students hold multiple responsibilities including occupational commitments."
- Early intervention (the review's catch-them-early theme) is the leading recommendation – "theme emerged frequently as a leading recommendation"
- Early-semester data identify at-risk students (Raju D. & Schumacker R., 2015; Colorado J. T. & Eberle J., 2010) – "Mining and analyzing pre-college and beginning-semester data is useful in identifying and predicting at-risk students"
- Early alert system in the LMS (Shaw et al., 2016) – "typically found in the Learning Management System"
- Retention is defined inconsistently (Haydarov R., Moxley V. & Anderson D., 2013) – "There appears a notion in regard to the inconsistent use and interpretation of the term retention among institutions offering online programs."
- Lee Y. & Choi J. (2011; Educational Technology Research and Development, 59(5); S101) group dropout factors into student, course/program and environment factors that interact – "factors from students, course/program, and environment influence each other"
- Multiple stakeholders are needed – "we recommend a collective effort from multiple stakeholders when addressing retention issues in online learning"

## Reliability

Primary (systematic literature review); authors Pauline Salim Muljana and Tian Luo, Old Dominion University; Journal of Information Technology Education: Research, 18, 19–57 (Informing Science Institute, peer-reviewed, open access). Citation traction as of 2026-11-05: 113 Crossref citations, 244 OpenAlex citations. The percentage gaps are cited from older US studies (2009–2011), not the authors' own data.

## Open questions

The 8–14% and 10–20% gaps come from US community-college and US institutional reports from 2009–2011; a more recent and non-US figure is needed (see other Part 1 sources). The review does not report when in the semester students leave.
