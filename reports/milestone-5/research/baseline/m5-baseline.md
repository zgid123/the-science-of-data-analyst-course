## Milestone 1: Business Goal (Section 3.1)

Source: `reports/milestone-1/report/03-01-business-goal.md` (SHA-256 f99753ab586754e3)

## 3.1. Business Goal

The project's Business Goal is to **improve successful course completion in the program's online and blended courses
by identifying students who stop taking part early enough to support them within the semester**.

The goal names an outcome for the organisation, more students passing their courses, rather than an activity such as
analysing LMS data or a technical solution such as a prediction model. The brief marks this difference as the test of
a suitable Business Goal, and its own example of a suitable goal also names early identification of students for
timely support [1]. The second half of the goal states when the outcome must be pursued, within the semester, because
every stakeholder placed the useful moment for support in the first weeks (Section 2.3).

The goal comes from three stakeholders. The program manager wants to know early how many students really complete,
and wants students supported to meet the standard rather than a better-looking rate (PM1, PM2). The support office
wants to reach students before they reach the academic warning list (AD1). The lecturer wants students who start the
course to finish it (CL2). The goal matters to students, for whom a failed course costs a semester's time and fees,
and to the university, whose withdrawal rate is tied to its enrolment quota [3].

If the goal is pursued, it supports two decisions in every semester, which Section 7 classifies: whom lecturers and
the support office contact first at each review point, and where the program manager places the time of the support office and the lecturer-advisors across
courses and groups. A third decision, whether to extend the approach beyond a pilot, depends on the results.

## Milestone 1: SMART Goal (Section 3.2)

Source: `reports/milestone-1/report/03-02-smart-goal.md` (SHA-256 d7b32a609f5b39a2)

## 3.2. SMART Goal

The SMART Goal fixes what is measured, for whom and by when. Its figures need one assumption, stated first. The
scenario gives no real completion rate for the university, and the brief asks that any baseline not taken from real
data be marked as an assumption rather than presented as a fact [1]. The proposal therefore assumes a baseline
completion rate of 70%. Before any target is committed, this assumed figure is replaced by the real rate, recounted
from the university's records for the same courses, as the program manager asked (VPM8).

> **SMART Goal.** Raise the course completion rate of students enrolled after the add-drop period in the pilot
> courses from their recounted baseline (assumed 70%) by five percentage points (assumed target 75%) by the end of
> the 2027–2028 academic year.

The pilot courses are about four of the program's online and blended courses, with about 600 enrolments in total
(both assumptions), chosen before the pilot begins in Semester 2 of 2026–2027. The data office can support only a few courses with a consistent setup at first (VDO4), and
fixing the courses in advance prevents the target from being met by changing which courses are counted. Their
completion rate is compared with that of similar courses outside the pilot, so that a good result cannot come simply
from choosing easy courses (VPM9).

The baseline is the completion rate of the same courses in 2024–2025 and 2025–2026, recounted under Circular
83/2026 so that approved leave and transfers do not count as non-completion (VPM8). The contact process runs in the
pilot courses from Semester 2 of 2026–2027 through the 2027–2028 academic year, and the target is the completion rate
pooled over the pilot courses in 2027–2028, because the program manager doubted that five points could be gained in one semester
(VPM5) and accepted the goal in this longer form (VPM7).

The target is demanding but achievable. At a baseline of 70%, 30 of every 100 enrolments end without a pass, so five
more passes per 100 enrolments means helping five of those 30 students to pass, about one in six. The pilot is small
enough for weekly capacity: at the assumed baseline, about 180 of 600 enrolments end without a pass, while the support
office (about 30–40 contacts a week) and the lecturer-advisors of the pilot classes (three to five each) can reach well
over 100 students across the two review points (VAD6, VAD7). Published gains from
early-warning programs are contested [19], so the target remains an assumption, to be reviewed after the pilot. The
goal is relevant because it measures the Business Goal directly and answers the needs of the program manager and the
support office for real completion and earlier contact (PM2, AD1). Appendix VII (Table A.18) checks the goal against
each of the five SMART criteria.

The SMART Goal measures progress, not the effect of contact. Students who are contacted may have passed anyway, as the
support officer warned (VAD4), so a rise in completion shows that the situation has improved but not why. Whether contact
itself raised completion needs a comparison design, which Section 7 discusses.

## Milestone 1: KPIs (Section 3.3)

Source: `reports/milestone-1/report/03-03-kpis.md` (SHA-256 7698548222c8f943)

## 3.3. Key Performance Indicators

The brief allows one primary KPI and at most two secondary KPIs, each with a reason why it measures progress towards
the SMART Goal [1]. The primary KPI measures the SMART Goal itself. The two secondary KPIs measure whether the support
process behind the goal works, which matters because completion moves slowly and is known only at the end of a
semester.

**Primary KPI: course completion rate.** The number of enrolments in the pilot courses that end in a pass, divided by
the number of enrolments after the add-drop period, pooled over the pilot courses. A fail and a withdrawal from the
course both count as non-completion. Approved leave and transfers to another program leave the denominator, as under
Circular 83/2026 [4]. This KPI is the SMART Goal's own measure and the outcome that the program already reports, so it
tracks the goal directly. It is read at the end of each semester from the academic management system.

**Secondary KPI 1: early-sign contact rate.** This KPI uses the early-sign rule, a simple and transparent definition
of a student who shows early signs of disengagement: a student who has missed two or more assessed tasks, or submitted
them late, by the end of week 4. The end of week 4 is the data cut-off of the week-5 review point: the last day whose
data may be used for that list, one week before it reaches staff, because exports from the LMS take about a week
(DO4). The KPI is the number of students meeting the rule who were contacted by
the end of week 5, divided by the number of students meeting the rule. It shows whether students who begin to
disengage are reached while support can still help, which is what the program manager and the lecturer asked for
(PM3, CL6), and it can be read in week 6, within the semester rather than after it. Students who meet the rule but are not on a
list, because capacity is full, count as not contacted, so a low value signals that capacity is too small rather than
that staff failed; the KPI is monitored without a target until the pilot shows what is achievable.

**Secondary KPI 2: follow-up rate.** The number of students on a contact list who were contacted within seven days of
the list being sent, divided by the number of students on the list. It shows whether the lists fit staff capacity and
are actually used. A list that staff cannot follow up is worse than no list, as the data officer warned (DO10), and a
list that is too long loses the support office's trust (AD10). The lecturer's wish to act on a short list every week
(CL11) is met only if this rate stays high. It is read one week after each review point. Appendix VII (Table A.19)
summarises the three KPIs with their timing and data sources.

Both secondary KPIs depend on a contact record, a shared log of who contacted which student, when, how and with what
result. The university has no such record today: the lecturer asked for one (VCL4), and the support officer
described today's alerts from lecturers as unrecorded (VAD1). Section 6 therefore adds it to the data requirements. Differences between student groups, such as first-year and later-year students, are
not a separate KPI; they are handled as a fairness limit in the prescriptive question AQ4 (Section 4.1).

## Milestone 1: Questions at four levels (Section 4.1)

Source: `reports/milestone-1/report/04-01-four-level-questions.md` (SHA-256 300389d938bb7457)

## 4.1. Questions at Four Levels

The four questions form one path from understanding the situation to supporting action, in which each question hands
its result to the next. Appendix VIII shows how each question meets the requirements the brief sets for its level.

**AQ1 – Descriptive.** In the program's online and blended courses over the four semesters of 2024–2025 and
2025–2026, what share of students enrolled after the add-drop period passed, failed or withdrew, by course, delivery
mode and year of study, and how does the week of the first missed assessed task compare between students who passed
and students who did not?

**AQ2 – Diagnostic.** Which signs observable in weeks 1–4 (missed or late quizzes and assignments, LMS activity
compared with the course's own average, missed tasks in several courses at once, and, in blended courses only,
attendance at in-class sessions) show the largest difference in later non-completion rates between students who have
the sign and students who do not, and are these differences similar across courses, delivery modes and years of
study?

**AQ3 – Predictive.** For each student enrolled in a pilot course, how well can failing or withdrawing by the end of
the semester be estimated from the data recorded by the end of week 2 and by the end of week 4, compared with a simple
rule such as "two or more assessed tasks missed"? Accuracy is measured on a list as long as staff can follow up each
week, as recall and precision, and is compared between first-year and later-year students and across courses.

**AQ4 – Prescriptive.** At each review point (weeks 3 and 5), which students should be listed, in what order, and with
which contact (a message from the lecturer or lecturer-advisor, a call from the support office, or a referral to the
student affairs office, which handles financial and personal matters, separate from the support office), so that the list reaches as many as possible of the
students most likely not to complete? The list must fit weekly capacity (three to five contacts per lecturer-advisor,
about 30–40 for the support office), reach a precision of at least 50%, and keep first-year recall within ten points
of later-year recall.

**How the questions connect.** AQ1 shows which courses and groups lose most students and in which weeks
non-completers begin to fall behind, which tells AQ2 where to look. AQ2 compares the signs named in its wording, each
taken from the interviews, and asks which differ most rather than which cause non-completion, because records of what
students did cannot show why they did it. AQ3 tests whether those signs identify students in time. Its unit is one
student's enrolment in one course, because a student may struggle in one course and not in another, and it may use
only data up to the data cut-offs at the end of weeks 2 and 4. Recall is the share of students who later fail or
withdraw who appear on the list, and precision is the share of listed students who later fail or withdraw; an estimate
that does no better than counting missed tasks adds complexity without value. AQ4 turns the AQ3 ordering into a list
for each review point. Its precision limit keeps staff from spending most of their time on students who are fine
(CL8), and its fairness limit keeps the list from missing first-year students (VPM4); both limits are assumptions, to
be reviewed once the data audit of Milestone 2 shows what is achievable. Each level prepares the next without proving
a cause, and the audit may show that only part of this path can be answered with the university's data.

## Milestone 1: Decision supported and value (Section 7)

Source: `reports/milestone-1/report/07-00-decision-value.md` (SHA-256 19086def5d5cabde)

# 7. Decision Supported and Expected Value

The analysis of Sections 4–6 serves three decisions, made by different people at different levels of the university.
This section classifies each decision, explains how its level sets the evidence the project must provide, and states
the value the project is expected to bring to each group of people involved.

**Three ways of classifying a decision.** The brief asks for three classifications [1]. The level of a decision is
strategic planning, management control (the brief's tactical level) or operational control, following Anthony's
levels as presented by Gorry and Scott Morton [24]. A decision is programmed, in Simon's sense, when it is routine and
has a worked-out procedure [24]. It is a one-way door when it is nearly irreversible and needs careful deliberation,
and a two-way door when it can be reversed and taken quickly [25]. Appendix IX gives the full definitions.

Table 7.1 applies the three classifications to the three decisions and states the evidence each decision needs.

**Table 7.1. Decisions supported and their classification**

| Decision | Who decides, using what | Level | Programmed? | Door | Evidence needed |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| D1. Whom to contact at each review point, by whom and with which offer | Lecturer-advisors and the support office, using the AQ4 list | Operational control (operational) | Programmed: repeated at every review point, with a fixed procedure | Two-way: a contact that does not help can be followed by another | A list that reaches more later non-completers than the simple rule (AQ3); a member of staff checks each name |
| D2. Where to place the time of the support office and the lecturer-advisors each semester | The program manager, using AQ1, AQ2 and the KPIs | Management control (tactical) | Semi-programmed: repeated each semester, but with judgement about courses and staff | Two-way: staff can be moved again next semester | A pattern repeated over several semesters, correct records and agreement with staff (PM7) |
| D3. Whether to extend the approach to all courses and link data routinely | University leadership, legal staff and the data office | Strategic planning (strategic) | Non-programmed: taken once, with no earlier case to follow | Close to one-way: it changes the university's data practice and students' trust | Pilot results, legal approval, and AQ1 and the KPIs for pilot and comparison courses (VAD4, VPM9) |

**How the level of a decision sets the evidence needed.** D1 is frequent and reversible, so it can start with the simple
missed-task rule checked by staff, and the AQ3 ordering replaces the rule only if it reaches more later non-completers
on a list of the same length; if a contact does not help, the next review point corrects it. D2 moves
scarce staff time between courses, and the program manager will move staff only for a pattern that repeats rather
than one bad semester or one difficult lecturer (PM7), so it needs results from several semesters. D3 commits the
university's data practice and the trust of its students, and it would be hard to reverse once records are linked
routinely and staff rely on the lists. It therefore needs the strongest evidence: AQ1 and the KPIs measured for pilot
and comparison courses before and after the pilot, legal approval, and an honest account of what the pilot did not
show. Whether contact itself caused any change in completion needs a design that compares contacted and similar
uncontacted students, which the evaluation plan of Milestone 4 sets out.

**Expected value.** Each group of people involved stands to gain something specific. Students who fall behind are
contacted while catching up is still possible, by someone they know and with a practical offer such as an extension
or a short pause (ST5, ST6). Lecturers and the support office spend their limited time on the students who most need
it, with a reason for each name and a record of what happened (VCL3, VCL4). The program manager sees which courses and
groups lose students during the semester, and can place support time where it is needed instead of spreading it
evenly (PM6, VPM3). The university gains the student-level status records that Circular 83/2026 calls for [4]. Even a
negative pilot result has value: it shows, at small scale and before any one-way commitment, which part of the path
from description to action does not work.

## Milestone 2: AQ–Data Requirement Matrix (Section 2.1)

Source: `reports/milestone-2/report/02-01-aq-data-matrix.md` (SHA-256 afab980d8a17717c)

## 2.1. AQ–Data Requirement Matrix

**What every question shares.** AQ1–AQ3 share one unit of analysis: one student's enrolment in one course in one
semester, so that a student who struggles in one course and not in another is seen correctly. AQ3 takes this unit at
each data cut-off, and AQ4 lists students, one entry per student across courses. The population
is the students still enrolled after the add-drop period, which is also the denominator of the primary KPI. The
outcome is the final result in three classes: any passing grade counts as completion, while a fail and a withdrawal
from the course count as non-completion. A withdrawal must carry its date, because AQ3 must know whether a student was
still enrolled at each data cut-off, the last day whose data a contact list may use.

**What each question needs.** AQ4 is judged by recall, the share of students who later fail or withdraw who appear on
a list, and precision, the share of listed students who later fail or withdraw. Table 2.1 is the brief's requirement
matrix. For each question it states the unit, what
must be measured, the variables the question cannot do without, the time window and grain, and the decision point at
which the answer is used.

**Table 2.1. AQ–Data Requirement Matrix**

| AQ | Analytics type | Unit of analysis | Outcome / measure | Required variables | Time / granularity | Decision point |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| AQ1 | Descriptive | Student–course–semester | Pass, fail or withdrawal; week of the first missed task, for students who pass and for those who do not | Persistent student, course and semester identifiers; course start date; enrolment start and end dates; final result; dated withdrawal (academic management system); task due and submission dates (learning platform) | Four semesters; week | D2, end of semester |
| AQ2 | Diagnostic | Student–course–semester with weekly signs | Difference in later non-completion between students with and without each sign | AQ1 variables, plus dated activity (learning platform); missed and late tasks (only tasks due before a student's withdrawal); courses taken in the same semester (academic management system) | Weeks 1–4; day or week | D2; reasons in D1 |
| AQ3 | Predictive | Student–course–semester at each data cut-off | Non-completion by the end of the semester | Timestamped activity and submissions (learning platform); dated withdrawal, enrolment start date and background known at enrolment (academic management system) | Cut-offs at the end of weeks 2 and 4; at least three semesters in time order | D1, weeks 3 and 5 |
| AQ4 | Prescriptive | Student at each review point | Recall and precision within capacity; outcome after contact | AQ3 output, one entry per student; weekly capacity (an assumption; staff rosters); contact record (to be created) | Each review point and week | D1, whom to contact |

**Optional variables.** Some variables enrich the questions without deciding whether a dataset can serve: delivery
mode, year of study, attendance, a status that separates leave and transfer from withdrawal, and the assignment of
students to lecturer-advisors. A dataset without them can still answer the core of each question; the clauses that
rely on them are then covered only in part (Partial coverage, Section 7), rather than the dataset being rejected.

**Must-have gates.** From the required variables, we derived six must-have gates, conditions that any candidate
dataset must meet whatever its score, numbered G1 to G5 with G2 in two parts:

(1) G1 Outcome: a final result for each enrolment that separates pass, fail and withdrawal, with the date of each withdrawal, because the primary KPI and AQ3's target depend on it.
(2) G2a Dated assessed tasks: due dates and dated submissions, counted from the course start, because missed and late tasks are the main early signs of AQ2 and the basis of the first secondary KPI.
(3) G2b Dated activity: activity on the learning platform for each student by day or week, because AQ2 and AQ3 compare activity in the first weeks.
(4) G3 History: at least three runs (separate offerings) of the same courses, ordered in time, with a later run available for testing, because AQ3 must be tested on a later semester than the one it learns from.
(5) G4 Access: an open license for academic use, official documentation and verifiable provenance, because the brief forbids data of unclear origin or without a right of use [1].
(6) G5 Privacy and linkage: anonymised data with a student identifier that persists across tables and runs, because the questions join results, tasks and activity for the same student.

**Why OULAD.** OULAD is selected because it is the only candidate whose documentation meets the timing and license

facts open, and the scores count only what is documented. Documentation scores 1, for example, because the provider
page gives no version number and its download link was broken; only the figshare deposit carries a version. The selection was therefore made conditional on the files
confirming three facts: that the result values separate pass from fail (G1), that each module has enough runs for
testing (G3), and that a student's identifier stays the same across runs (G5). Section 6 confirms the first and the
third. It shows the second met for five of the seven modules, which keeps OULAD under the rule of Section 4.1 and
narrows AQ3 to those runs; the rejected candidates have a single period or no dates, so they miss the history gate
outright.

**Limits that come with the choice.** OULAD describes UK distance-learning modules from 2013–2014, and its modules were
selected by the publisher partly because many students failed them [3], so its failure and withdrawal rates are likely
to exceed those of a typical program. It holds no delivery mode, year of study, attendance, leave or transfer status.
Its authors also advise analysing module runs that start in February and in October separately [3], which limits how
many comparable runs each module has.

**No supplementary dataset.** The brief allows at most one supplementary dataset when it is really needed [1]. None is
selected: no candidate holds the missing contact, capacity or attendance data, and the only other source of dated
activity, the XuetangX data, lacks a license and a pass or fail outcome [8].

## Milestone 2: Acquisition and provenance (Section 5)

# 5. Data Acquisition and Provenance

**Principle.** The brief requires the raw data to stay exactly as published and the collection to be repeatable by
anyone [1]. The selected dataset was therefore collected by a script that downloads the published files, checks them
and stores them unchanged, and that records everything needed to obtain them again. Two kinds of evidence make the copy
checkable. A checksum is a short fingerprint computed from a file's exact bytes; if a single byte changes, the
checksum changes. We recorded two of them for every file: SHA-256 (Secure Hash Algorithm, 256-bit), the
standard used for verification, and MD5 (Message Digest 5), the checksum that the provider itself publishes. The files
are stored as comma-separated values (CSV), plain-text tables that any tool can read. Table 5.1 is the brief's
acquisition plan for OULAD.

**Table 5.1. Data acquisition plan for OULAD**

| Item | Value |
| :---- | :---- |
| Dataset title | Open University Learning Analytics Dataset (official title) |
| Provider / authors | J. Kuzilek, M. Hlosta and Z. Zdrahal, Knowledge Media Institute, The Open University [3] |
| Official URL / DOI | Provider page [2]; data descriptor doi:10.1038/sdata.2017.171 [3]; files deposited on figshare, doi:10.6084/m9.figshare.5081998.v1 [3] |
| Access date | 6 November 2026 (Vietnam time, UTC+7) |
| Version / release | figshare version 1; the provider page offers the latest version without a number [2] |
| License / terms | CC BY 4.0 [2], [12] |
| Acquisition method | Direct download of one zip archive from the figshare deposit; size and SHA-256 checked against reference values written in the collection script; the seven tables extracted unchanged |
| Raw files | anonymisedData.zip (46,750,706 bytes) containing courses, assessments, vle, studentInfo, studentRegistration, studentAssessment and studentVle (CSV, 453.8 MB for studentVle) |
| Integrity evidence | SHA-256 and MD5 of the archive and of each table in the manifest, the list of files with their checksums; all seven tables matched their pinned SHA-256 |
| Citation | Kuzilek, Hlosta and Zdrahal (2017), *Scientific Data*, 4, 170171 [3] |

**Which copy, and why.** OULAD is published in three places: the provider's own server, whose link was broken
when the data were collected [2]; a figshare deposit with a DOI and a version number, which the data descriptor cites
[3]; and the UCI repository [12]. We collected and cite the figshare deposit, and a row-by-row comparison found
the UCI copy identical apart from how missing values are written (Appendix III).

**Reproducible in two commands.** Anyone with the Data Collection Package can re-create our copy with two
commands. The first, `src/collect.py`, downloads the archive, checks its size and checksum and extracts the seven
tables. The second, `src/verify_structure.py`, runs the structural checks of Section 6. The package also holds the
manifest of checksums, the data dictionary copied from the descriptor, the source and license sheet, the citation and
the collection log. The raw files stay as published: nothing is cleaned, filtered or converted. Appendix III shows the
package's layout and its source, license and manifest tables.

Five changes follow from these ratings, and each narrows how a question is measured without changing what it asks.
The population rule (C09) counts an enrolment as enrolled on a given day when the student registered by that day, or
the registration day is unknown, and did not unregister by it; it removes the 58 late registrants from the Milestone 2

## 4.2. Data Gap Decisions and Synthetic Data

**Kinds of gap.** The brief separates three kinds of gap [1]. A missing value is a variable that exists but is empty
for some records, which can be analysed, kept as a category or imputed with a reason. A missing variable or concept is
something the dataset never collected, which must not be imputed or generated as if it were real; it calls for another
valid source or a revised question. A missing target means no outcome for supervised prediction. OULAD adds a fourth
case that looks like missing data but is not: a recorded absence, where a missing row itself records an event, such as
no click or no submission. Table 4.2 sorts the gaps of OULAD into these classes with the handling of each.

**Table 4.2. Missing values, recorded absences and missing variables**

| Class | Cases in OULAD | Evidence | Handling |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Missing value | imd_band (1,111); registration day (45); TMA score (173); withdrawal day of 93 Withdrawn; due day of 11 exams | DQ04, DQ08, DQ11–DQ13 | "Missing" category for imd_band; indicators; no imputation in the prepared views; filling fitted on training runs only in model matrices; exams left out of task counts |
| Recorded absence | No click row; no submission row; empty unregistration day of students who stayed | DQ17; data descriptor | Zero counts after left joins; an empty unregistration day means the student did not withdraw |
| Missing variable | Year of study, delivery mode, attendance, leave or transfer status, contacts and their outcomes, staff capacity, the day a TMA mark was released | C04, C05, C08 | Not imputed or simulated; questions narrowed (AQ1–AQ3) or the contact part deferred (AQ4) |
| Missing target | None: final_result is present for every enrolment | Section 2.2 | Supervised target available |

**Adding another dataset.** The third step of the procedure, adding a compatible open dataset, does not close any
gap. None of the seven candidate datasets screened in Milestone 2 records contacts, attendance or year of study for
the same students, and joining a dataset of other students only because it shares a variable name is what the brief
rules out [1]. The questions were therefore narrowed and the contact part of AQ4 deferred, which is the brief's order
of priority [1].

**Synthetic data.** Of the five levels of synthetic data in the brief's policy, this project uses only level A, small
labelled test data for code [1]: a few hand-made test enrolments in the `tests/` folder of the Prepared Data Package
check that each feature formula gives the expected value. They never enter a prepared view, a statistic or a finding,
and no augmentation is planned; Appendix IV sets out the five levels and their conditions.

## 1.1. Goal, Decisions and Analytical Questions

**Goal and measures.** The Business Goal is to improve successful course completion in a university program's
online and blended courses by identifying students who stop taking part early enough to support them within the
semester. The specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound (SMART) Goal turns this into a target: the
completion rate of students enrolled after the add-drop period, the first weeks in which students may still register
for or drop a course, rises in the pilot courses from a baseline that the pilot will recount from the university's
records, assumed for now to be 70%, by five percentage points to 75% by the end of the 2027–2028 academic year.
That completion rate is the primary key performance indicator (KPI). Two secondary KPIs follow the contacts: the share
of students with early signs of disengagement who are contacted by the end of week 5, and the share of students on a
contact list who are contacted within seven days.

**Decisions.** The proposal supports three decisions. D1 is whom the lecturer-advisors and the support office contact
at each review point, a moment in weeks 3 and 5 when a contact list reaches staff; each list uses the data up to its
data cut-off, the end of week 2 for the week-3 list and the end of week 4 for the week-5 list. D2 is where the
program manager places the time of the support office and the lecturer-advisors each semester, and D3 is whether to
extend the approach to all courses after the pilot.

**Data and changes.** The data are the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset (OULAD), an open stand-in for the
university's own records: 22 runs of seven Open University (OU) modules from 2013 and 2014 [2]. A module is an OU course; its modules carry
anonymised codes, AAA to GGG, and each offering of a module, a module run, is named by its year and its start month, B
for February and J for October [2]. Milestone 3 prepared the data as enrolments, each one student in one module run. Milestones 2 and 3 recorded thirteen changes, C01 to C13, in the
Controlled Change Log, the list of every change to the proposal with its reason in the data. They set the population
to the OULAD module runs and time to days from the start of each run, with the two cut-offs at day 13 and day 27; they
replace comparisons by delivery mode, which OULAD does not vary because every run is distance learning, and year of study
with comparisons by module and run; and they restate how two
questions are measured, AQ3 (C10) and AQ4 (C13), as described below. The Business Goal, the SMART Goal and the three decisions are unchanged.

**The questions as they stand.** The brief allows only the analytical questions (AQ) that remain valid after the AQ–Data Fit of Milestone 3, the step
that rated each question against the real data, to be modelled, and a part that was deferred may not return without a recorded change [1].

## 4.1. Evaluation Design Cards

**What a card holds.** The brief asks for an Evaluation Design Card for each question with seven items: the primary
and secondary metrics, the validation strategy, the baseline, the error analysis, the fairness or subgroup check and
the success criterion [1]. The metric follows the decision it supports: recall matters when a contact is cheap and
missing a struggling student is costly, precision when a contact is costly [23], and when only a limited number of
top-ranked cases can be acted on, performance among those cases is the practical measure [24].

**Capacity and the main operating point.** The capacity k of a run is 3 or 5 contacts per tutor group, with a group
assumed to hold 20 students, that is 15% or 25% of the enrolments registered at the cut-off. The main operating point
is 5 per 20 at day 27, because it serves the list delivered in week 5 and is close to the share of students the early-sign rule flags at day 27: 28.8% of the 27,535 enrolments registered then
across all 22 runs, a share computed without any outcome. A confusion matrix of listed and unlisted students against
their outcome is reported for each run; at a fixed list length its cells give recall, precision and lift, which
replace the F1 score, because the list length is set by capacity rather than chosen to balance the two kinds of error.
The Brier score that the AQ3 card lists is the mean squared difference between predicted risk and outcome.

**Metric definitions.** The cards use metrics that are defined once here, for a run r with n enrolments, the set Y of
its non-completers, base rate b = |Y|/n, and a list L of the k highest scores. In words: recall at capacity is the
share of the run's non-completers who are on the list; attainable recall is the most a list of length k can reach,
which is below 1 when the run has more non-completers than places on the list; normalised recall divides recall by
that ceiling, so that 1 means the best list possible; precision is the share of the list who do not complete; and
lift divides precision by the base rate, so that 1 means no better than chance. Appendix II (Table A.4) gives the formula of every metric.
