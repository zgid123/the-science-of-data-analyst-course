# Draft A – Speech script by the Storytelling with Data method

**Talk.** Early Identification of Disengaging Students in Online Courses – team N04, MTH045 final presentation,
about 25 minutes, 30 slides.

**Big idea (core message).** Staff notice disengaging students only when it is too late to help, and the obvious sign,
a missed task, comes too late for most of them; on two years of open course data, a short early-sign rule that also
counts inactivity names every student with a factual reason by week 4, so the university should test it as a
staff-checked contact list in a randomised pilot rather than assume it works.

**Arc (Dykes data-storytelling arc).**
1. Setting and hook (Slides 2–5): students are noticed at week 5 or 6, after the two-to-four-week window for help;
   the university wants a short list with a reason beside each name.
2. Rising points, data (Slides 6–10): no Vietnamese data exist, so we chose OULAD as a stand-in and rated each
   question against it.
3. Rising points, preparation and method (Slides 11–18): two data facts reshape the plan (the day-14 add-drop rule,
   an early-sign rule with an activity part); a frozen design judges lists fairly within capacity.
4. Aha moment (Slides 19–24): 45.3% do not complete, yet under one in five of them has missed a task by week 4; all
   five early signs, inactivity included, go with non-completion in both years.
5. Solution and next steps (Slides 25–30): the rule becomes the candidate list for D1, a hypothesis for D2, a method
   for D3; the AQ3 test, a recount and a randomised pilot come next.

## Method notes (SWD)

- **WHO.** The two lecturers (Dr. Nguyễn Trần Minh Thư, MSc. Phạm Minh Tú), who grade whether the analytics chain
  holds from the problem to the decision, and our classmates. They are analytically literate (rates, intervals,

- **WHAT.** One takeaway: *a missed task comes too late; an early list must also count inactivity, and its use must

- **HOW.** Live 25-minute group talk with slides, then questions. The presenter narrates, so slides stay sparse:
  one idea per slide, one lead sentence, at most one chart, every key and term on the slide.

### Big Idea (one sentence)

Staff at the university notice disengaging students only when help is late (situation), and the obvious warning
sign, a missed task, reaches under one in five non-completers by week 4 (complication), so an early list must also
count inactivity and its use must be tested in a randomised pilot before it becomes routine (action).

### The 3-minute story

At a Vietnamese university moving online, lecturers are sure a student has gone quiet only around week 5 or 6, the
program manager sees counts after the semester and the support office meets these students on the warning list, yet
help works best within two to four weeks. Staff asked for a short list at weeks 3 and 5 with a reason beside each
name. We turned that need into a goal, three decisions and four questions, from description to a ranked list. No
Vietnamese course data are open, so we chose OULAD, 22 runs of seven UK distance-learning modules, the only candidate
with dated withdrawals, dated tasks and daily clicks. Two data facts reshaped the plan: we count only students still
registered at day 14, the end of add-drop, and few tasks fall due in the first weeks, so the early-sign rule also
counts a week without activity. We fixed the evaluation before running anything: train on 2013, test once on 2014,
judge a list against capacity and against the rule, audit fairness. Then the evidence: 45.3% of 28,061 enrolments did
not complete, the same in both years; most non-completers do miss a task eventually, but under one in five by week 4.
All five early signs, inactivity included, go with later non-completion in both years, a missed task by about
40 points. So the rule is the candidate list for whom to contact, a hypothesis for where support time goes and a
method for whether to extend. The model test, a recount on the university's records and a randomised pilot of
contact come next; these are associations, not causes.

## Slide 2 – Noticed Too Late   [Problem]   (~50 seconds)
**Lead:** Staff notice a student who has stopped taking part only after the window in which help works best.
**On the slide:** Window for effective support: weeks 2–4 [M5 §1]. Lecturers become sure: around week 5 or 6
[M5 §1]. Program manager sees counts: after the semester [M5 §1]. Support office meets the student: on the academic
warning list at the end of the semester [M5 §1]. Disengagement (stopping taking part): a student misses a task, opens

**Visual:** timeline of weeks 1–8 and semester end; the 2–4 window shaded in the accent colour, the three notice
points in grey to its right (sequence: the gap between "could help" and "does notice" is the story).
**Speaker notes:** Picture a student in an online course. She misses one assignment, logs in less, then goes quiet.

these students on the academic warning list, at the very end. But published work places the window for effective

records the university already holds show the drift earlier, with a reason staff can trust? First, who decides what.

## Slide 5 – Four Analytical Questions   [Problem]   (~50 seconds)
**Lead:** Four questions climb the analytics levels, and each feeds a decision.
**On the slide:** Unit: one student's enrolment in one module run [M5 §3]. AQ1 descriptive – what share pass, fail or
withdraw, and in which week non-completers first miss a task (D2, D3). AQ2 diagnostic – which signs by week 4 go with
later non-completion, within each module; association only (D2; reasons for D1). AQ3 predictive – how well

tested on 2014 (D1). AQ4 prescriptive – who ranks first on a list of fixed capacity, with what gain over a random list,

**Visual:** a four-step ladder, descriptive to prescriptive, each step labelled with its decision (sequence).
**Speaker notes:** The three decisions need evidence at four levels, all about one unit: one student's enrolment in
one course run. AQ1 is descriptive: how many complete, and when do non-completers first miss a task? AQ2 is
diagnostic: which signs by week 4 go together with later non-completion? We say "go with" on purpose; it is an

## Slide 11 – Who Counts: the Add-drop Rule   [Preparation]   (~45 seconds)
**Lead:** We count only students still registered at day 14, because that is when registration closes on OULAD.
**On the slide:** University rule: completion counts students enrolled after the add-drop period [M5 §1.2]. OULAD
proxy: an enrolment counts when it is still registered at day 14, because registration closes two weeks after a run
starts [M5 §2.1]. Same rule for every population: registered at day 14 for the completion rate (28,061 enrolments),
at days 13 and 27 for the two lists [M5 §2.1, §6]. Late registrants are flagged, not removed [M5 §6.1].
**Visual:** a day axis from day 0 to day 27 with three markers (day 13, day 14, day 27) and the population size at
day 14 (sequence).
**Speaker notes:** The first change concerns who counts. At the university, the completion
rate counts only students enrolled after the add-drop period, because those who leave during add-drop never really
started. OULAD has no add-drop field, but registration closes two weeks after a run starts. So an enrolment counts if
it is still registered at day 14. That leaves twenty-eight thousand sixty-one enrolments, and the same logic at day 13
and day 27 defines the populations of the two lists. This looks like bookkeeping, but you will see that this one rule
can move a run's rate by more than ten points. The second change concerns the early sign itself.
**Sources:** university rule [M5 §1.2]; day-14 proxy and population [M5 §2.1]; populations at days 13 and 27 [M5 §6,

## Slide 12 – The Early-sign Rule   [Preparation]   (~50 seconds)
**Lead:** Few tasks fall due in the first weeks, so the early-sign rule also counts a week without activity.
**On the slide:** Early-sign rule: by the data cut-off, a TMA or CMA due but not submitted, or a TMA submitted late,
or no platform activity in the previous seven days [M5 §2.1]. Why: a task other than the exam falls due by day 27 in
only 16 of 22 runs [M5 §5]; no 2013 run has a task due by day 13, 7 of 9 have one by day 27 [M5 §6.1]. Late quizzes
stay out of the rule: counting them would raise the share flagged at day 27 from 28.8% to 41.2% [M5 §6.1]. One rule
on OULAD and in the pilot [M5 §2.1]. At day 27 the rule flags 28.8% of 27,535 enrolments [M5 §2.1].
**Visual:** simple text: "16 of 22 runs" have any task due by day 27, with the rule's three parts as three icons below,
the activity part in the accent colour (single key number).
**Speaker notes:** In Milestone 1 the interviews told us the first sign is usually a missed quiz or assignment, and
our first rule counted missed tasks. The data said no. By day 27, only sixteen of twenty-two runs have any task due at
all, and in 2013 no run has one due by day 13. A task-only rule simply cannot fire in many runs. So the final
early-sign rule has three parts: a task due and not submitted, an assignment submitted late, or no platform activity
in the last seven days. Late quizzes stay out, because counting them would raise the flagged share from twenty-eight
point eight to forty-one point two percent. Next, keeping the future out.
**Sources:** rule definition, share flagged, one rule [M5 §2.1]; tasks due by day 27 [M5 §5]; 2013 runs and late
quizzes [M5 §6.1].

**Speaker notes:** Preparation rested on three decisions: nothing recorded is deleted or imputed, every feature uses

## Slide 15 – Judging a Contact List   [Method]   (~50 seconds)
**Lead:** A list is judged by how many later non-completers it reaches against the most a list of its length could
reach.
**On the slide:** Capacity: 3 or 5 contacts per 20 students, that is 15% or 25% of a run; main point: 5 per 20 at
day 27 [M5 §9, glossary]. Recall at capacity: share of a run's non-completers who are on its list [M5 §9]. Normalised
recall: recall divided by the most a list of that length can reach; the ceiling ranges from 46% to 100% between
training runs [M5 §9]. Lift: share of non-completers on the list divided by the run's base rate; 1 means chance
[M5 §9]. Base rate: share of a run's enrolments who do not complete [M5 glossary]. Why a ranking: the rule names 28.8%
at day 27, more than a list of 15% or 25% can hold [M5 §8].
**Visual:** worked example with 20 student icons, 9 non-completers marked in the accent colour, a list of 5; caption
computes recall, ceiling and normalised recall for this illustrative group (the icons are an illustration, not data).
**Speaker notes:** Staff can contact only a few students, so capacity fixes the list length: three or five per twenty
students. A toy example, just for the arithmetic: twenty students, nine will not complete. A list of five reaches at
most five of the nine. If four of our five are right, recall is four of nine, but against the ceiling it is four of
five: that is normalised recall. We need it because the ceiling ranges from forty-six to one hundred percent between
runs. Lift compares the list with chance. Why rank at all? Because the rule alone names twenty-eight point eight
percent at day 27, more than any list can hold. So when is a model good enough?
**Sources:** capacity, metrics, ceiling range [M5 §9]; rule share and need for ranking [M5 §8].

## Slide 16 – Validation and the Success Gate   [Method]   (~50 seconds)
**Lead:** The model is built on 2013 and scored once on all 13 runs of 2014, under a protocol frozen in advance.
**On the slide:** Development: leave-one-run-out folds on the 9 runs of 2013; a challenger replaces the primary model
only with a gain of at least 0.03 in pooled normalised recall and in 7 of 9 folds [M5 §9.1]. Test: the frozen model
scored once on the 13 runs of 2014; 9 paired and 4 unpaired runs also reported apart [M5 §9.1]. Gate S1: the model's
pooled normalised recall must exceed the rule list's, with the paired bootstrap interval above 0 and a sign-flip test
p below 0.05 [M5 §9]. Frozen protocol: evaluation choices fixed in advance so the test runs are scored once
[M5 glossary]. Probabilities shown only if calibration holds [M5 §9.1].
**Visual:** two blocks, "2013: build and choose" (grey) and "2014: score once" (accent), with an arrow and a lock icon
(sequence).
**Speaker notes:** The key word here is "once". A model is built and chosen only on the nine runs of 2013, holding out
one run at a time. A more complex challenger wins only with a gain of at least 0.03 in normalised recall, in at least
seven of nine folds. Then the frozen model is scored once on all thirteen runs of 2014, including a module it has
never seen. Success is gate S1: beat the early-sign rule's list, with an interval above zero and a test below 0.05. If
it fails, the simple rule stays. Every choice was fixed before any 2014 outcome was used. A good list also has to be

**Sources:** folds, challenger rule, test runs, calibration [M5 §9.1]; gate S1 [M5 §9, Table 9.1].
