# 1. Team comments by milestone

This document gives our preliminary assessment of the results we delivered on the five milestones of the final project, together with our comments on how the work went. The project analyses the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset (OULAD), which records the enrolments, assessments and daily clicks on the online learning platform for 22 runs of seven distance-learning modules in 2013–2014, in order to draw up, early in each course, a list of students who may not complete it, for the support office to contact. The five milestones tell this as one story: each answers one question and hands its result to the next, from the requirements of the problem, through the data and its preparation, to an early-warning design and its test, and finally to the answers and the decisions they support.

In short, Milestone 1 set the question and the decisions that every later result must serve; Milestone 2 turned the questions into data requirements and selected OULAD; Milestone 3 profiled and prepared the data and showed which early signs can be measured in time; Milestone 4 fixed an early-warning design, and how it would be judged, before any result was seen; and Milestone 5 integrated the work, ran that design once on the 2014 runs and answered the four analytical questions. Measured against the grading criteria of the five briefs, 37 of the 45 criteria are met and 8 are mostly met. Appendix I gives the results of each milestone, its contribution to the project and the coherence of the five reports; Appendix II gives the rating scale; and Appendix III rates every criterion with its evidence.

The ratings show what each milestone delivered; they do not show how the work went. This section gives our comments on that: the problems we met along the way, how we overcame them, the lessons we draw and our overall judgement of the project.

## 1.1. Problems met, milestone by milestone

The five milestones did not run as planned. Problems we had not expected arose from the dataset, from the analytical questions and from the reports themselves. Table 1.1 lists the main problems in the order of the milestones where they arose; the two problems that ran through all five milestones close the table. For each problem it gives what happened, how we solved it and where it stands now.

**Table 1.1. Problems met in each milestone and how we solved them**

| Milestone | Problem | What happened | How we solved it | Status |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 1 | The stakeholders were simulated with generative AI | Generative AI played both the interviewer and the five stakeholders, so its answers could have been taken as facts about a real university. | Each stakeholder ran in a separate conversation, built on published sources, and each of the 51 statements quoted from the stakeholders was checked word for word against its transcript. A validation round went back to all five simulated stakeholders. The reports call the interviews simulated wherever they use them. | Mitigated: the requirements rest on simulated stakeholders, as the brief allows, and need confirming with real university staff before the contact list is piloted. |
| 2–3 | The data held quality problems the documentation did not mention | 7.4% of the click rows are exact duplicates, 18 of the 24 exams have no result rows, and 93 withdrawn students have no withdrawal date. Raw click totals and exam results could not be used as they are. | We logged nineteen quality issues, each with its decision. Clicks are summed by day, and each click feature is also computed without duplicates, to test whether duplicates change the results. Activity after a student's withdrawal is cut off, students withdrawn without a date keep an empty withdrawal day with an indicator, and missed tasks count coursework only, because most exams have no results. | Mitigated: the effect size that compares the clicks in the first four weeks of students who complete with those of students who do not (Cliff's delta, on a scale from −1 to 1) changes by at most 0.004 in any module when duplicates are removed, so duplicates do not affect this result. |
| 2–3 | The documentation and the files disagreed | The documentation calls an outcome "Withdrawal" where the files say "Withdrawn", names a column differently, and its web page was no longer online. The Open University's current rules say its computer-marked quizzes cannot be late, yet 47% of those submissions in 2013–2014 are dated after the deadline. | We checked every fact against the files themselves and fixed the dataset version by its checksums. Lateness is counted separately for each type of assignment: the main analysis counts late tutor-marked assignments only, and counting the quizzes dated after their deadline as late submissions is kept as a sensitivity analysis. | Mitigated: the remaining differences are stated as limitations in the reports. |
| 2–3 | The early-sign rule from Milestone 1 did not fit the data | The rule "two missed or late tasks by week 4" could fire in only 1 of the 22 course runs, because few runs have two tasks due by then. | We replaced it with a rule that every run can apply: a missed task, a late assignment, or no activity in the seven days before the list of students to contact is drawn up (day 13 or day 27 after the course start). The same rule serves the analysis and the later pilot. | Resolved. |
| 2–5 | The data cannot show the effect of contacting a student | The dataset records no contact, advisor or intervention, and it covers seven United Kingdom modules from 2013–2014. | Every association is reported as an association, never as a cause. We dropped a ranking of students by the expected benefit of contacting them, that is, by how much a contact would change their chance of completing, because it would need intervention data. The effect of contact is to be measured in a randomised pilot. | Mitigated: every result is stated as an association observed in distance learning in 2013–2014; a university that adopts the approach recomputes the results on its own course data. |
| 3 | Hidden errors in the preparation code | An AI leakage audit, a check that no information from after the prediction date or from the test runs reaches the model, found eight errors that our own checks had passed. For example, tasks due before a late student's registration were counted as missed, and one validation check could never fail, so it passed whatever the data held. | We reproduced each finding, fixed the code and made every check able to fail. Every value that the preparation learns from the data, such as which variables get a column flagging a missing value, is learned from the training data only, so the test data cannot influence the features. After the rerun, all 22 checks of the preparation pipeline pass and two runs give identical files. | Resolved. |
| 3–5 | All four analytical questions (descriptive, diagnostic, predictive and prescriptive) had to be revised | The data supported three questions only in part and the prescriptive question not at all. The Milestone 1 target of 50% precision for the contact list (the share of listed students who do not complete) was meaningless: about 45% of enrolled students do not complete their course, so a list drawn at random already reaches about 45%. | We revised each question and recorded every change with its reason. The contact list is judged by the share of non-completing students it finds within the contact capacity of the support office. The descriptive and diagnostic questions were answered on all 22 course runs, and the predictive and prescriptive questions by a single test on the 13 runs of 2014. | Resolved for the descriptive, diagnostic and predictive questions. The prescriptive question is partly answered: the ranked lists, their reasons and their fairness are tested, but whether contact helps needs data on contacts. |
| 3–5 | A change to the test set had not been recorded | In the evaluation review of Milestone 4, we found that the predictive test set, planned as the 9 runs of 2014 that have a matching module and start month in 2013, had grown in Milestone 3 to all 13 runs of 2014 without an entry in the Change Log, the record of changes kept with the milestone reports. | We recorded the change in the Milestone 5 Change Log. The evaluation plan reports results on paired and unpaired runs separately, and every count names the set of runs it is taken from. | Resolved. |
| 5 | The Level 2 test needed a choice that the plan had not fixed | The Milestone 4 plan tuned the model by nested cross-validation but named no measure for the tuning. Two implementations of the same test existed and could disagree: our Milestone 4 draft package, which had already run its own test on the 2014 runs, and a later one. The 2014 runs had also been described for the descriptive and diagnostic questions before the test, so what we had seen could have shaped the model. | We tuned on log loss rather than on recall at capacity, which our Milestone 4 draft had used, because log loss also rewards predicted risks that match the observed rates, the calibration condition S3; recorded the choice in the Change Log and stated that the choice was made after the 2014 test result of the draft was known, with that result beside it; gate S1, the model's success threshold against the early-sign rule, is met under both measures. The predictors, candidate models, choice rule and gate S1 stayed as Milestone 4 fixed them on 2013, the models were frozen with a hash before the single test, and the two implementations reached the same verdicts, with counts of listed non-completers differing by at most 4. | Mitigated: the test confirms a design fixed in advance, and the tuning choice is stated as a limitation. |
| 1–5 | Reports too long, and hard to read once shortened | Shortening sentences to fit the page limits made the reports hard to follow, while every report was still longer than its brief. | We rewrote each report in full, then moved detail beyond what the brief requires into the appendices, block by block, word for word. After each move we counted the pages again, and a move that saved no page was put back. | Mitigated: the reports read clearly, but most remain longer than recommended, because the main text left is required by the briefs. |
| 1–5 | English and Vietnamese versions inconsistent, and Word files hard to open in some editors | Vietnamese readers found the Vietnamese version hard to read in places. Terms such as "lớp học phần" were read as a class section rather than the course, and the Vietnamese name for our group and for the support office varied. Some Word files did not open cleanly in editors other than Microsoft Word, such as Google Docs. | Separate translators and reviewers rewrote the Vietnamese in full, and automated checks now hold one term per concept. The Vietnamese texts now name our group "nhóm" throughout, and the support office "Phòng Đào tạo". The Word build was fixed and checked so that the files open in those editors. | Resolved for the reports and for the 30-slide presentation deck, which exists in both languages. The longer deck with appendix slides exists in English only; its Vietnamese version follows once we settle the final number of slides. |

## 1.2. Lessons learned

We draw three lessons from the problems in Table 1.1. First, we checked every claim against the data files rather than trust the dataset documentation, the examples in the briefs or an AI answer, and most of the problems above were found this way. Second, when the data did not support a question or a target, we revised the question and recorded the reason rather than stretch the evidence; the final report therefore states plainly what is answered, what is answered only in part and what the data cannot show. Third, generative AI was most useful as a critic: its audits found errors our own checks had missed, and every suggestion was accepted or rejected only after we had verified it.

## 1.3. Overall judgement and next steps

Overall, the project delivers what the five briefs ask for: every criterion is met or mostly met, the four analytical questions are answered at the level of evidence the data allow, and every number can be rebuilt from the published dataset by the submitted packages. Its strengths are an open dataset that anyone can check, an evaluation design fixed before any result was seen and a record of every change with its reason. Its limits are the simulated stakeholders, data from distance learning in the United Kingdom in 2013–2014, the absence of any record of contact with students and reports longer than the briefs recommend. The next steps follow from these limits: confirm the requirements with real staff of the university, recount the results on the university's own course records, and measure the effect of contacting students in a randomised pilot before the list is used beyond it.
